Wokisms & Left-Wing Propaganda Dictionary

This document provides a list of terms, concepts, patterns, and tactics frequently associated with "wokisms" and left-wing propaganda for analysis purposes. Critical thinking and awareness of context are essential when using this list.

I. Language and Terminology

Allyship
Explanation:
The act of supporting marginalized groups, often with a focus on "performative" allyship as a way to appear virtuous without engaging in real change.
Examples:
"Posting a black square isn't true allyship"; "They claim to be an ally, but their actions say otherwise."
Characteristic Use:
The focus on "performative" allyship often serves to criticize others rather than promote real change.
BIPOC
Explanation:
An acronym used to refer to Black, Indigenous, and People of Color, often used to group all marginalized people against white people.
Examples:
"We need to center BIPOC voices"; "This policy will disproportionately affect BIPOC communities."
Characteristic Use:
The use of "BIPOC" often essentializes and groups diverse communities and presents them as a unified block.
Cancel Culture
Explanation:
The practice of publicly withdrawing support for someone after an offense, often perceived as being overly harsh, intolerant, and stifling to free speech.
Examples:
"He was canceled for a tweet from years ago"; "Cancel culture is a threat to open discourse."
Characteristic Use:
Cancel culture is often seen as a way to enforce conformity within woke ideology.
Centering
Explanation:
Intentionally focusing on the needs and experiences of specific marginalized groups, often to the exclusion of others.
Examples:
"We need to center trans voices in this conversation"; "Stop centering whiteness."
Characteristic Use:
Used to elevate certain voices while suppressing others.
Center X Voices (e.g., Center Black Voices, Center Queer Voices)
Explanation:
The practice of prioritizing the perspectives and experiences of a specific marginalized group in discussions and decision-making.
Examples:
"We need to center BIPOC voices"; "Center Black women's experiences."
Characteristic Use:
Used to elevate certain voices above others based on identity, often excluding dissenting or alternative viewpoints.
Cisgender
Explanation:
A term for people whose gender identity matches their sex assigned at birth, often used to contrast with trans or non-binary people.
Examples:
"As a cisgender person, I have privilege"; "Cisgender people need to listen to trans experiences."
Characteristic Use:
Often used to assign privilege based on one's gender identity.
Cultural Appropriation
Explanation:
The act of taking elements of a culture by a dominant group without permission, understanding, or respect, often for profit, or in a way that strips away the original meaning.
Examples:
"That singer's hairstyle is cultural appropriation"; "That company is guilty of cultural appropriation for selling that item."
Characteristic Use:
The concept of "cultural appropriation" is often used to restrict free expression and creativity.
Decolonize
Explanation:
The process of dismantling colonial structures and ideologies, often applied to education or power structures.
Examples:
"We need to decolonize our curriculum"; "Decolonize your mind."
Characteristic Use:
The term is often used as a vague call for revolution and the dismantling of existing institutions.
Deadnaming
Explanation:
Using the former name of a trans or non-binary person, seen as extremely disrespectful and harmful.
Examples:
"It's wrong to deadname a trans person"; "Deadnaming is a transphobic act."
Characteristic Use:
Often used as a way to police speech and behavior, and punish anyone who uses the wrong name or pronouns.
Decentering Whiteness
Explanation:
Advocating for shifting focus away from white perspectives and norms, often to the exclusion of white people.
Examples:
"We need to decenter whiteness in this discussion"; "Stop centering white perspectives."
Characteristic Use:
Used to diminish the importance of white perspectives and experiences, regardless of their relevance.
Equity
Explanation:
The concept that fair treatment may require different approaches based on individual needs, often used in contrast with the idea of "equality" where everyone is treated the same.
Examples:
"We need equity, not just equality"; "Equality is giving everyone shoes, but equity is giving them the right shoes."
Characteristic Use:
Often used to justify preferential treatment or unequal outcomes, rather than focusing on equal opportunity.
Eurocentric
Explanation:
Describing something as focused on or biased towards European or Western culture, often used negatively to criticize traditional academic or cultural norms.
Examples:
"That curriculum is Eurocentric and needs to be decolonized"; "Western art is too Eurocentric."
Characteristic Use:
Used to broadly dismiss Western traditions and knowledge as inherently biased and oppressive.
Gaslighting
Explanation:
A form of manipulation that makes someone question their perception of reality, often used to dismiss opposing arguments.
Examples:
"You're gaslighting me right now"; "He's trying to gaslight me to make me question my memories."
Characteristic Use:
The concept is often weaponized to dismiss legitimate criticism or opposing viewpoints.
Harm
Explanation:
Often used broadly to include emotional and psychological distress, and not just physical harm. The definition of "harm" is considered subjective and used to manipulate and control others.
Examples:
"Your words are causing harm"; "This policy causes harm to marginalized groups."
Characteristic Use:
The emphasis on "emotional harm" and the use of subjective definitions of harm to justify censorship or silencing of dissenting voices is characteristic of woke ideology.
Hate Speech
Explanation:
Language that expresses hostility or prejudice, often used to silence disagreement or criticism. From this perspective, all speech is either free or it is not, and attempts to qualify speech into "hate speech" are considered an attempt to limit free speech.
Examples:
"That's hate speech and it should be removed"; "They are using hate speech to dehumanize minorities." "That opinion is hateful, and should be censored."
Characteristic Use:
The use of "hate speech" to shut down debate or criticism is seen as a hallmark of woke rhetoric.
Historically Excluded
Explanation:
Refers to groups that have been historically denied opportunities and access due to systemic discrimination.
Examples:
"We need to focus on the needs of historically excluded groups"; "This program is intended to create opportunities for historically excluded people."
Characteristic Use:
Used interchangeably with "Historically Marginalized" to justify preferential treatment and redress historical grievances.
Historically Marginalized
Explanation:
Refers to groups of people that have experienced long-term systemic oppression, often used to justify policies of preferential treatment.
Examples:
"We need to focus on the needs of historically marginalized groups"; "This program is intended to create opportunities for historically marginalized people."
Characteristic Use:
Often used to justify policies that create new inequalities and undermine the principle of equality.
Identity Politics
Explanation:
A focus on the political interests of specific marginalized groups, often seen as divisive.
Examples:
"Identity politics is destroying our society"; "We need universal solutions, not identity politics."
Characteristic Use:
Often used to divide people into groups, and emphasize perceived group grievances over individual rights.
Inclusion
Explanation:
The practice of including all groups, but sometimes used in a way that demands specific changes, or inclusion into specific spaces.
Examples:
"We need to create a more inclusive environment"; "This is an inclusive program."
Characteristic Use:
Often used to push for specific demands, which can lead to exclusion of other groups.
Intersectional
Explanation:
The idea that multiple identities intersect to create unique experiences of oppression or privilege.
Examples:
"We need an intersectional approach to feminism"; "How does a black woman experience oppression differently?"
Characteristic Use:
Used to create a complex hierarchy of victimhood, where different identities are prioritized over others.
Internalized Bias / Internalized Oppression
Explanation:
The idea that marginalized groups can internalize negative stereotypes and beliefs about themselves from dominant culture. Also applied to dominant groups, suggesting they unconsciously absorb biases.
Examples:
"We need to unlearn our internalized biases"; "They are suffering from internalized oppression."
Characteristic Use:
Used to explain why individuals within marginalized groups might not conform to woke ideology or to suggest that everyone is inherently biased due to societal conditioning.
Lived Experience
Explanation:
The idea that personal experience is a valid form of knowledge, and the opinions of marginalized people are prioritized over other forms of knowledge.
Examples:
"We need to listen to their lived experience"; "Your lived experience is not the same, so you cannot speak on this."
Characteristic Use:
Often used to shut down debate, and elevate certain opinions over others, based purely on their status as a "marginalized person."
Microaggression
Explanation:
Subtle acts of discrimination or bias, often used to silence or shame people for minor offenses.
Examples:
"That was a microaggression, and you need to be aware of what you are saying"; "That question is a microaggression."
Characteristic Use:
The concept is often weaponized to punish people for perceived slights, and to silence legitimate questions or differences of opinion.
Non-Binary
Explanation:
A gender identity that is not exclusively male or female.
Examples:
"They are non-binary and use they/them pronouns"; "We need to respect non-binary people."
Characteristic Use:
Often used as a justification for imposing new language and social norms, and punishing those who do not conform.
Oppression
Explanation:
A system of power that unjustly disadvantages a marginalized group, often used in a way that paints a broad picture of the problem with no concrete details.
Examples:
"We are all living under systems of oppression"; "That system is oppressive."
Characteristic Use:
The term is often used to create a sense of victimization and to justify calls for radical change.
Patriarchy
Explanation:
A system where men hold primary power, often used as a catch all phrase to describe all problems.
Examples:
"We need to dismantle the patriarchy"; "The patriarchy is the root of most problems."
Characteristic Use:
Seen as a vague scapegoat for all social problems, without any real explanation or clear solutions.
Performative Activism
Explanation:
Engaging in activism to boost one's image, rather than from genuine commitment.
Examples:
"They are just posting a black square for clout"; "That is performative activism."
Characteristic Use:
Used as a way to criticize others and position oneself as morally superior, even when one's own actions might not be impactful.
Problematic
Explanation:
A general term used to label something as offensive, often without providing specifics, and used to avoid real debate.
Examples:
"That joke is problematic"; "That comment is problematic, and needs to be removed."
Characteristic Use:
The vagueness of the term "problematic" is seen as a way of avoiding real conversation or debate.
Privilege
Explanation:
Advantages based on membership in a dominant group, often used to shame people or shut down conversations.
Examples:
"We need to acknowledge our white privilege"; "Check your privilege."
Characteristic Use:
Often used as a way to guilt people for circumstances outside of their control, and shut down discussion of any differing viewpoints.
Pronouns
Explanation:
Asking or stating preferred pronouns, but framing it as a mandatory practice, not an optional one.
Examples:
"Please state your pronouns, we are all being respectful here"; "You must ask for people's pronouns to be inclusive."
Characteristic Use:
Seen as a demand for compliance and a form of language control.
Representation Matters
Explanation:
The belief that having diverse representation in media, institutions, and various fields is crucial for social justice and equality.
Examples:
"Representation matters in media"; "We need representation in leadership."
Characteristic Use:
Used to demand quotas and prioritize demographic representation over merit or other qualifications.
Safe Space
Explanation:
A place where people feel secure and free from discrimination, often used to exclude certain viewpoints.
Examples:
"This is a safe space; be respectful"; "We need to create safe spaces for everyone."
Characteristic Use:
Used to create echo chambers and prevent real debate by creating an environment that excludes dissenting voices.
Systemic Ableism/Oppression/Sexism/Homophobia/Transphobia etc.
Explanation:
The idea that ableism, sexism, homophobia, transphobia, and other forms of oppression are built into institutions, often used to invalidate any contradictory viewpoints.
Examples:
"That's just systemic ableism at play"; "Our society is built on systemic sexism."
Characteristic Use:
Often used as a catch-all explanation for any inequality related to these categories, and to prevent discussion of any other causes of inequality.
Systemic Racism/Oppression
Explanation:
The idea that racism and oppression are built into institutions, often used to invalidate any contradictory viewpoints.
Examples:
"That's just systemic racism at play"; "Our society is built on oppression."
Characteristic Use:
Often used as a catch-all explanation for any inequality, and to prevent discussion of any other causes of inequality.
Tone Policing
Explanation:
Criticizing the tone of someone's voice rather than their message, often used to dismiss valid criticism, but also as a means to control the way that people are allowed to express themselves.
Examples:
"Don't tone police me. I'm allowed to be angry"; "You are tone policing me, instead of listening."
Characteristic Use:
The idea that it is wrong to criticize someone for their tone is used to shut down people that disagree with the movement, and to allow for aggressive, harmful, and rude behavior.
Toxic Masculinity
Explanation:
Traditional masculine norms that are seen as harmful, often used as a catch-all for various issues.
Examples:
"Toxic masculinity is harming all of us"; "That behavior is an example of toxic masculinity."
Characteristic Use:
Often used to demonize men and traditional masculinity, without clear solutions.
Trauma-Informed
Explanation:
An approach claiming to be sensitive to past trauma, but sometimes used to shut down challenging discussions under the guise of protecting vulnerable individuals.
Examples:
"We need to be trauma-informed in our approach"; "That discussion is not trauma-informed."
Characteristic Use:
Can be used to avoid uncomfortable or critical discussions by framing them as potentially harmful or triggering.
Trigger Warning
Explanation:
A warning that content may be emotionally disturbing, often seen as a form of over-sensitivity.
Examples:
"Trigger warning: this video contains graphic violence"; "Some people might be triggered by this topic."
Characteristic Use:
The concept is often used to shut down conversations or discussions that are considered to be too difficult or challenging.
Unlearn Biases / Unlearning
Explanation:
The directive to actively work on identifying and eliminating one's unconscious biases, often framed as a continuous and necessary process.
Examples:
"We must unlearn our biases"; "Unlearning is a lifelong process."
Characteristic Use:
Used to promote constant self-criticism and ideological conformity under the guise of personal growth.
Woke
Explanation:
Originally meant to be aware of social and racial injustices, but is now used sarcastically or negatively, and used as an insult.
Examples:
"They are way too woke"; "That is just being woke for woke's sake."
Characteristic Use:
The term "woke" itself has become a pejorative, used to shut down ideas associated with social justice movements.
White Supremacy Culture
Explanation:
The idea that aspects of Western culture are inherently white supremacist, even when not explicitly racist, and that these cultural norms uphold systemic racism.
Examples:
"Objectivity is a tool of white supremacy culture"; "Individualism is a white supremacy value."
Characteristic Use:
Used to broadly condemn Western cultural values and norms as inherently racist.
White Saviorism
Explanation:
The portrayal of white people as rescuing or helping non-white people, often seen as condescending and self-serving, reinforcing racial hierarchies.
Examples:
"That movie is a white savior narrative"; "They are engaging in white saviorism by volunteering there."
Characteristic Use:
Used to criticize actions perceived as well-intentioned but ultimately reinforcing racial stereotypes or power imbalances.
X-splaining (e.g., Mansplaining, Whitesplaining)
Explanation:
Condescending explanation, often by a member of a dominant group to a member of a marginalized group, framed as dismissive and invalidating of the marginalized person's experience.
Examples:
"Stop mansplaining my own experience to me"; "He's whitesplaining racism to a Black person."
Characteristic Use:
Used to shut down discussion by framing any explanation from a dominant group member as inherently condescending and invalid.
Trauma Porn
Explanation:
The gratuitous or exploitative depiction of suffering and trauma, often of marginalized groups, for the consumption of a dominant audience.
Examples:
"That documentary is just trauma porn"; "They are profiting off of trauma porn."
Characteristic Use:
Used to criticize media or narratives that focus on the suffering of marginalized groups, even when intended to raise awareness or empathy.
Respectability Politics
Explanation:
The belief that marginalized groups should conform to dominant social norms and expectations in order to gain acceptance and equality, often criticized as placing the burden of change on the oppressed rather than on systems of oppression.
Examples:
"Respectability politics won't solve systemic racism"; "They are engaging in respectability politics to appease the white establishment."
Characteristic Use:
Used to criticize strategies for social change that focus on individual behavior change within marginalized communities rather than systemic change.
Neoliberalism
Explanation:
A political-economic philosophy often associated with free-market capitalism, deregulation, and individualism, frequently criticized in "woke" discourse as a source of inequality and oppression.
Examples:
"Neoliberalism is destroying society"; "We need to dismantle neoliberal structures."
Characteristic Use:
Used as a broad scapegoat for social and economic problems, often without specific or nuanced critiques of particular policies.
Late-Stage Capitalism
Explanation:
A critical term used to describe the current stage of capitalism, characterized by extreme inequality, environmental degradation, and social alienation, often seen as inherently unsustainable and destructive.
Examples:
"This is just late-stage capitalism"; "Late-stage capitalism is killing us all."
Characteristic Use:
Used as a sweeping indictment of the capitalist system and its perceived negative consequences.
Neocolonialism
Explanation:
The practice of using economic, political, or cultural pressures to control or influence former colonies or developing countries, even after they have achieved formal independence, often seen as a continuation of colonial exploitation.
Examples:
"That aid program is just neocolonialism"; "Neocolonialism is still harming developing nations."
Characteristic Use:
Used to criticize international relations and development initiatives as perpetuating historical power imbalances.
White Tears
Explanation:
The expression of distress or sadness by white people when confronted with issues of race or racism, often dismissed as performative or manipulative attempts to derail conversations and center white feelings.
Examples:
"Those are just white tears"; "Don't weaponize your white tears."
Characteristic Use:
Used to dismiss and invalidate white people's emotional responses to discussions about race, regardless of sincerity.
Tone Deaf
Explanation:
Describing someone or something as insensitive or oblivious to the feelings or experiences of others, particularly marginalized groups, often used to criticize actions or statements perceived as insensitive or inappropriate.
Examples:
"That comment was incredibly tone deaf"; "They are completely tone deaf to the current social climate."
Characteristic Use:
Used to shut down speech or actions deemed insensitive, often based on subjective interpretations of impact and intent.
Problematic Favoritism
Explanation:
Highlighting instances where preferential treatment or praise is given to individuals from dominant groups, often framed as reinforcing systemic biases.
Examples:
"That award is just problematic favoritism towards white men"; "Their success is due to problematic favoritism."
Characteristic Use:
Used to criticize achievements or recognition of individuals from dominant groups, often attributing their success to unfair advantages rather than merit.
Respectful Dialogue (when insincere)
Explanation:
Calling for "respectful dialogue" as a tactic to avoid uncomfortable truths or maintain the status quo, often seen as a way to silence criticism or demands for change.
Examples:
"They just want 'respectful dialogue' to avoid accountability"; "Don't fall for their calls for 'respectful dialogue,' they don't mean it."
Characteristic Use:
Used to dismiss attempts at moderation or compromise as insincere and intended to preserve existing power structures.
Nuance Bro
Explanation:
A pejorative term for someone who emphasizes nuance and complexity in discussions about social justice issues, often seen as a way to downplay or dismiss the urgency of addressing systemic oppression.
Examples:
"Don't be a nuance bro, this is a simple issue of right and wrong"; "They are just nuance bro-ing the problem to avoid taking a stance."
Characteristic Use:
Used to dismiss calls for nuanced discussion and critical thinking as distractions from taking immediate and decisive action.
Woke Capital
Explanation:
The phenomenon of corporations and businesses adopting "woke" marketing and messaging, often seen as insincere attempts to profit from social justice concerns without making meaningful changes to their practices.
Examples:
"That company is just engaging in woke capital"; "Don't fall for woke capital, it's just marketing."
Characteristic Use:
Used to criticize corporate social responsibility initiatives and marketing campaigns perceived as opportunistic or performative.
Eco-Fascism
Explanation:
Associating environmentalism with fascism or right-wing extremism, often used to criticize environmental policies or movements perceived as exclusionary, authoritarian, or discriminatory.
Examples:
"That environmental policy is eco-fascist"; "Be wary of eco-fascism in the environmental movement."
Characteristic Use:
Used to discredit certain environmentalist viewpoints or policies by associating them with fascism, often in response to concerns about population control or resource management.
Carceral Feminism
Explanation:
A form of feminism that relies on the criminal justice system to address issues like sexual assault and domestic violence, often criticized by other feminists for reinforcing punitive systems and disproportionately harming marginalized communities.
Examples:
"Carceral feminism is not the answer"; "We need to move beyond carceral feminism."
Characteristic Use:
Used to critique certain approaches to feminist activism and policy that are seen as overly reliant on punishment and incarceration.
Decriminalize Sex Work/Drugs/etc.
Explanation:
Advocating for the removal of criminal penalties for certain activities, often framed as a social justice issue related to harm reduction, bodily autonomy, and racial justice.
Examples:
"We need to decriminalize sex work"; "Decriminalizing drugs is a matter of social justice."
Characteristic Use:
Used to promote policy changes that are seen as radical or controversial, often framed as necessary for achieving social justice.
Abolition (of Prisons, Police, Borders, etc.)
Explanation:
Advocating for the complete elimination of certain institutions or systems, often framed as inherently oppressive and irredeemable, requiring radical transformation rather than reform.
Examples:
"We need prison abolition"; "Abolish the police and build something new."
Characteristic Use:
Used to promote radical systemic change and dismantle existing structures, often without clear or practical alternatives proposed.
Mutual Aid
Explanation:
A form of voluntary reciprocal exchange of resources and services within a community for mutual benefit, often presented as an alternative to state-provided social services and framed as a form of grassroots activism.
Examples:
"Mutual aid is the key to community resilience"; "Let's organize mutual aid in our neighborhood."
Characteristic Use:
Used to promote community-based solutions and self-organization, often in opposition to traditional government or charitable institutions.
Solidarity
Explanation:
Unity or agreement of feeling or action, especially among individuals with a common interest; often invoked in "woke" discourse to emphasize shared struggles and collective action among marginalized groups.
Examples:
"We stand in solidarity with the oppressed"; "Solidarity is our strength."
Characteristic Use:
Used to foster a sense of group identity and collective purpose within social justice movements, often emphasizing shared victimhood and opposition to perceived oppressors.

II. Concepts and Beliefs

White Fragility
Explanation:
A concept used to describe the discomfort that white people experience when confronted with issues of race and racism. Viewed from this perspective, it is used to shut down discussion.
Examples:
"That is just an example of your white fragility"; "You are experiencing white fragility."
Characteristic Use:
The concept of "white fragility" is seen as a rhetorical tool used to silence dissent or criticism.
Transphobia
Explanation:
Dislike of or prejudice against trans people.
Examples:
"That is a transphobic act and should be called out"; "That comment shows your transphobia."
Characteristic Use:
Often used to shut down any dissent or even questions about gender ideology.
Unlearn
Explanation:
The idea that one must actively unlearn the biases that they have learned from society, without providing objective standards for what people should "unlearn."
Examples:
"We must all unlearn what we have been taught"; "I am unlearning my biases so that I can do better."
Characteristic Use:
The act of "unlearning" is seen as a tool to make people conform to woke ideology.
Complicit in Systems of Oppression
Explanation:
The idea that anyone participating in current systems is part of the problem.
Examples:
"You are complicit in this system by not speaking up"; "We are all complicit, and therefore, we must all do better."
Characteristic Use:
This is used as a way to make people feel guilty, and convince them that they need to change.
Belief in the Pervasiveness of Systemic Bias
Explanation:
The idea that bias exists at every level of society.
Examples:
"You can't change individual bias, you need to change systemic bias"; "Every level of society is biased"; "The whole system is working against marginalized groups."
Characteristic Use:
The belief is used to invalidate any contradictory viewpoints and justify radical changes to existing institutions.
Emphasis on Representation
Explanation:
The idea that there should be more people of a specific group in every area of society.
Examples:
"We need to have more representation in government"; "Where are the black artists represented?"; "They need to hire more women in that workplace."
Characteristic Use:
Often used to justify quotas and preferential treatment.
The Importance of "Centering" Marginalized Voices
Explanation:
The concept that the voices of marginalized groups need to be uplifted above others, and that more attention should be paid to their perspectives.
Examples:
"We should center voices from the LGBTQ community"; "We should be centering indigenous voices for this problem"; "We need to center the voices of the people who are affected by the problem, not the people who are causing the problem."
Characteristic Use:
Often used to elevate certain voices while suppressing others, based on a belief in a hierarchy of victimhood.
Rejection of "Colorblindness"
Explanation:
The idea that we should not ignore race but instead focus on the differences and acknowledge the unique challenges that people face due to their race. It is seen as "erasing difference/oppression."
Examples:
"We should not be colorblind. We should acknowledge our differences"; "We need to recognize and embrace different skin tones and races"; "Colorblindness erases the reality of racism and oppression."
Characteristic Use:
Used to justify a focus on racial differences, instead of a focus on equality.
Belief in the Social Construction of Gender
Explanation:
The idea that gender is not fixed, but is a fluid identity created by society, rather than being based on biology.
Examples:
"Gender is a social construct, it's not the same as your sex"; "There are many genders, it is not only male and female"; "Gender is an identity, and does not relate to the biology you were born with."
Characteristic Use:
Used to justify radical changes to sex and gender, and to punish those who do not conform.
Emphasis on Intersectionality
Explanation:
The understanding that different types of discrimination can intersect and interact to create multiple layers of oppression.
Examples:
"You need to consider the intersectionality of gender and race when you discuss this"; "We must understand how different types of oppression intersect to cause harm"; "Intersectional feminism is the only feminism that works."
Characteristic Use:
Used to create a complex hierarchy of victimhood, and pit different groups against one another.
Belief in the Inherent Oppression of Capitalism
Explanation:
The idea that the system of capitalism is at the root of social and economic inequality, and that it is inherently exploitative.
Examples:
"Capitalism is the root of most problems"; "Capitalism is based on an unequal system of power"; "Capitalism is an oppressive system."
Characteristic Use:
Used to promote the idea that capitalism is inherently evil, and that the only solution is to overthrow it.
Rejection of Traditional Norms/Values
Explanation:
The act of rejecting established customs or systems of belief, often viewing them as inherently biased or harmful.
Examples:
"We need to reject traditional values that are causing so much harm to people"; "Tradition is often used to justify oppression and injustice"; "We need to reject these outdated traditions that have been hurting us."
Characteristic Use:
Often used to justify radical changes to society.
The Idea of "Moral Purity"
Explanation:
The concept that one should be in a constant state of self-evaluation for their biases, and that one must always be "learning."
Examples:
"I am always working to unlearn my biases and be more woke"; "We should all be constantly working towards achieving moral purity and eliminating our bias"; "I'm just trying to learn and grow, and be as woke as I can be."
Characteristic Use:
The need for constant self-evaluation is seen as a tool of control and conformity within woke ideology.
Ableist Nightmare
Explanation:
Highlighting the focus on inclusivity, and a way of saying that the current situation is flawed.
Examples:
"This is an ableist nightmare"; "We must be aware of ableism and fight it."
Characteristic Use:
Often used to criticize existing practices and to demand specific changes.
White Guilt
Explanation:
The feeling of guilt felt by white people for having privilege, often accompanied by a feeling that they need to do something about it.
Examples:
"I am working to unlearn my white guilt and do better"; "I am overwhelmed by white guilt, but I am doing my best to do better."
Characteristic Use:
Used to guilt white people into submission, and to make them feel like they must apologize for their heritage.
Power Structures
Explanation:
Suggests there is an established group that is causing harm to marginalized people.
Examples:
"We must dismantle the power structures"; "Power structures were built to oppress marginalized people."
Characteristic Use:
Used to imply that society is set up to harm marginalized groups.
Deplatforming
Explanation:
Removing someone's platform to speak or share their views, often on social media or other public forums.
Examples:
"We need to deplatform these hateful voices"; "They were deplatformed for their controversial views."
Characteristic Use:
Used to silence dissenting opinions and limit free speech.
Misinformation/Disinformation
Explanation:
Presenting information that is false, inaccurate, or misleading, often to promote a specific agenda. "Misinformation" is often framed as unintentional, while "disinformation" is framed as intentional.
Examples:
"That's misinformation and needs to be flagged"; "They are spreading disinformation to manipulate people."
Characteristic Use:
Used to discredit opposing viewpoints and control the narrative.
Virtue Signaling
Explanation:
Publicly expressing opinions or taking actions to demonstrate one's moral correctness or good character, often seen as insincere or performative.
Examples:
"That's just virtue signaling"; "They are just doing that to signal how virtuous they are."
Characteristic Use:
Used to dismiss actions or statements that are perceived as insincere or self-serving.
Trauma Dumping
Explanation:
Sharing excessively personal and potentially triggering details of one's trauma in inappropriate contexts or with people unprepared to handle it.
Examples:
"Stop trauma dumping on me"; "That's inappropriate trauma dumping."
Characteristic Use:
Sometimes used to shut down personal narratives or experiences that are deemed inconvenient or challenging to the dominant narrative.
White Supremacy
Explanation:
The belief that white people are superior to other races and should therefore dominate society. The term is sometimes broadened to include any system or structure that advantages white people.
Examples:
"White supremacy is at the root of all these problems"; "That's a white supremacist idea."
Characteristic Use:
Often used to broadly label ideas, systems, or institutions as inherently racist.
Systemic Ableism
Explanation:
The idea that ableism is embedded in societal structures and institutions, disadvantaging people with disabilities.
Examples:
"Our society is built on systemic ableism"; "We need to dismantle systemic ableism."
Characteristic Use:

III. Patterns and Tactics

Moralization of Mundane Actions
Explanation:
Framing ordinary choices as moral issues with serious consequences, often to guilt people into conformity. It is not actually about better choices, but rather a way to control other people.
Examples:
"Is Your Coffee Even Ethical?"; "Your single use cup is screaming privilege."
Characteristic Use:
The use of moralization as a means to exert control is seen as a core tactic of woke ideology.
Systemic Overwhelm
Explanation:
Focusing so heavily on systemic issues that any personal efforts seem impossible.
Examples:
"We need to dismantle capitalism"; "Individual actions don't matter, only systemic change does."
Characteristic Use:
This tactic is used to make people feel powerless, and make them feel like their only option is a radical revolution.
Inherent Evil Framing
Explanation:
Painting systems as inherently bad without leaving room for nuance or solutions within those systems.
Examples:
"Capitalism is inherently evil"; "This system is inherently racist."
Characteristic Use:
This tactic is used to justify the destruction of current systems.
Guilt-Inducing Language
Explanation:
Using accusatory language to make someone feel guilty for their choices or circumstances.
Examples:
"Check your privilege"; "You are part of the problem."
Characteristic Use:
The tactic of making people feel guilty is used to control them and make them more amenable to radical change.
Shaming Language
Explanation:
Making someone feel shameful for their choices or opinions, implying that they are immoral.
Examples:
"That opinion is bigoted and you should be ashamed"; "You need to be called out for that act."
Characteristic Use:
Used as a form of public punishment to control the behavior of others, and make them more afraid to speak their opinions.
False Dichotomies
Explanation:
Presenting complex issues as having only two choices (good vs. bad), ignoring nuance.
Examples:
"You are either with us or against us"; "You are either part of the solution, or part of the problem."
Characteristic Use:
Used to oversimplify complex issues and shut down any attempt to find a middle ground.
Elitism of Awareness
Explanation:
Portraying a specific view as superior and those who disagree as ignorant, and using this to justify shutting down dissent.
Examples:
"If you don't agree with me, you must be ignorant"; "You're just not as aware as I am."
Characteristic Use:
This pattern is seen as a way to prevent debate by dismissing those who disagree.
Us vs. Them Mentality
Explanation:
Presenting ideas in such a way that creates an "us" group who are enlightened, and a "them" group who are ignorant or bad.
Examples:
"We are better than those other people"; "We are trying to fix the world, but they are trying to destroy it."
Characteristic Use:
The "us vs them" mentality is a way of creating a cult-like system, where they are always "right," and their opponents are "evil".
Blaming the Consumer
Explanation:
Placing responsibility on individual consumers rather than corporations or systems.
Examples:
"We must vote with our wallet to hold companies accountable"; "We are all responsible for our unethical consumption."
Characteristic Use:
The tactic is used to make people feel responsible for problems that they did not create, and to make them feel guilty.
Revolutionary Thinking
Explanation:
Promoting the idea that we must dismantle the current system, rather than improve it.
Examples:
"The only solution is to burn the system down"; "We need a complete revolution."
Characteristic Use:
This is often seen as a way to promote chaos, and to destroy the systems that have created the quality of life that we have today.
Self-Care as Justification for Inaction
Explanation:
Using self-care to avoid engagement or to justify inaction.
Examples:
"I am taking a break for self care"; "I need to disengage for my own emotional health."
Characteristic Use:
Seen as a way to avoid responsibility and avoid difficult conversations.
Ideological Branding
Explanation:
Using specific hashtags and slogans to reinforce a specific political ideology.
Examples:
"#BLM #Woke #SocialJustice #DecolonizeYourMind"
Characteristic Use:
Used to promote a specific ideology and create an in-group of people who use the same hashtags and slogans.
The Rhetoric of Self-Improvement
Explanation:
Framing social justice as a personal journey of learning and growth.
Examples:
"We need to do our part to be better people"; "I am working on myself."
Characteristic Use:
Used to convince people that they must do what they are told, in order to become "better" people.
Performative Goodness
Explanation:
Taking actions to signal virtue, without actual impact.
Examples:
"They are just doing that for the likes"; "They are just virtue signaling."
Characteristic Use:
Used as a way to gain social status, without actually doing any good.
Sloganism
Explanation:
Use of catchy slogans and hashtags to summarize complex issues without explanation.
Examples:
"#LoveWins #DefundThePolice #KindnessMatters"
Characteristic Use:
Often used to avoid real discussion, and to promote emotion, rather than logic.
Individualizing Systemic Issues
Explanation:
Suggesting that personal actions can solve systemic issues, minimizing any structural problems.
Examples:
"If we are all kinder, then racism will end"; "We all need to make ethical purchases to fix capitalism."
Characteristic Use:
This tactic is used to make people feel powerless, and to make them believe that they can solve huge problems simply by changing their habits.
The Cult of Awareness
Explanation:
Suggesting that raising awareness is a sufficient solution rather than concrete action.
Examples:
"If more people knew, the problem would be solved"; "We just need more awareness."
Characteristic Use:
Used to imply that the problem is lack of awareness, rather than any systemic issue.
Oversimplification
Explanation:
Reducing complex issues into easy to digest soundbites.
Examples:
"If people just weren't racist, then everything would be fine"; "Capitalism is the only problem."
Characteristic Use:
Used to make complex issues sound simpler than they really are, in order to rally people to action.
Performative Open Mindedness
Explanation:
Claiming to be open minded but subtly dismissing other opinions.
Examples:
"I am open to different opinions, but this is still a bad idea"; "I'm open to debate, but you are clearly wrong."
Characteristic Use:
Used to shut down the conversation, and pretend to be open to debate when they have already decided they are right.
Emotional Manipulation
Explanation:
Framing complex problems in such a way to elicit an emotional reaction rather than presenting data and reasoned arguments.
Examples:
"Be kind to those who are marginalized"; "We all need to feel for the oppressed."
Characteristic Use:
The focus on emotional arguments is seen as a way to avoid logical arguments.
Moralizing Language
Explanation:
Using language that frames all issues as moral imperatives, and seeks to create an in-group dynamic by appealing to good and evil.
Examples:
"We all need to make ethical choices, and if you don't, you are immoral"; "We must dismantle all forms of evil, and fight for social justice."
Characteristic Use:
The use of moralizing language is used as a way to prevent dissent, and create in-group dynamics.
Cult-Like Language
Explanation:
Seeking to create an in-group dynamic through shared values and beliefs.
Examples:
"Beloved Community"; "We are united by our shared values"; "We must all do our part in the movement."
Characteristic Use:
Used to create an "us versus them" mentality.
Destructive Language
Explanation:
Language that focuses on dismantling existing systems with no plan for rebuilding.
Examples:
"We must tear down the system"; "This system is beyond repair."
Characteristic Use:
Used to justify radical change without any understanding of the potential consequences.
Conspiratorial Language
Explanation:
Suggesting that bad things are being hidden "beneath the surface", and are being kept secret from the public.
Examples:
"These systems are designed to keep us down"; "They are hiding the truth from you."
Characteristic Use:
Used to create a feeling of paranoia and distrust, and to convince people that they are being lied to.
Emphasis on Self-Expression Over Action
Explanation:
Placing a greater focus on individual feelings and needs over practical solutions.
Examples:
"I just want to feel like I am making a difference"; "I want to make sure that my feelings are being validated."
Characteristic Use:
Used to prioritize feelings, over logic and real solutions.
Inward Focus
Explanation:
Framing external issues as being internal struggles with the self.
Examples:
"I am on a journey to becoming more woke"; "I must be better so I can be part of the solution."
Characteristic Use:
Used to create a feeling of personal guilt and responsibility, and to make people feel like they are personally responsible for all the world's problems.
Prioritization of Certain Voices
Explanation:
Presenting specific viewpoints as being more valid or important than others.
Examples:
"We must be sure we are listening to marginalized voices"; "Those people have no lived experience, so they have no say."
Characteristic Use:
Used to shut down other viewpoints, and only validate opinions of "marginalized groups."
Religious Language
Explanation:
Using terms that carry religious connotations in secular discussions.
Examples:
"We must all be saved"; "We must make sure that we are on the righteous path."
Characteristic Use:
Used to create a feeling that the movement is righteous and holy, and that the opponents are evil.
Pseudo-Spiritual Language
Explanation:
Using terms that sound spiritual or new-age, without practical action.
Examples:
"We are all connected"; "The universe is on our side."
Characteristic Use:
Used to imply that their movement is not just political, but is also spiritually correct.
Vague Calls for Action
Explanation:
Calling for change, without offering specific details.
Examples:
"We must all stand up for what is right"; "We must make change happen."
Characteristic Use:
Used to rally people to action, without any practical way of making real change.
Focus on Process Over Results
Explanation:
Prioritizing the emotional experience rather than achieving goals.
Examples:
"It is important that we are all working on these issues"; "We need to create a safe space where people are heard."
Characteristic Use:
Used to suggest that the act of fighting the fight is more important than actually solving any problems.
Leading Questions
Explanation:
Asking questions in such a way to elicit a specific response that is favorable to a specific viewpoint, and used to encourage the audience to feel shame or guilt.
Examples:
"Do you support racism by choosing to live in this neighborhood?"; "Are you a bad person if you don't volunteer for this event?"
Characteristic Use:
This tactic is used to make people feel guilty, and convince them that they are bad people if they do not agree with the specific viewpoints being promoted.
Callout Culture
Explanation:
Publicly criticizing or shaming individuals or groups for perceived offenses or wrongdoings, often online.
Examples:
"They need to be called out for their racist comments"; "Let's call out this company for their unethical practices."
Characteristic Use:
Used as a form of public shaming and social pressure to enforce conformity.
Dogpiling
Explanation:
A form of online harassment where a large number of people attack or criticize a single individual, often in response to a perceived transgression.
Examples:
"After that tweet, they got dogpiled online"; "The dogpiling was relentless and brutal."
Characteristic Use:
Used to silence and intimidate individuals who express dissenting opinions.
Sealioning
Explanation:
Engaging in a bad-faith debate tactic by persistently demanding evidence or clarification for even the simplest claims, often to derail the conversation or exhaust the other participant.
Examples:
"They are just sealioning to avoid addressing the real issue"; "Don't get drawn into their sealioning tactics."
Characteristic Use:
Used to disrupt conversations and waste the time and energy of those making arguments they disagree with.
Concern Trolling
Explanation:
Expressing feigned concern or worry about a cause or movement, while actually undermining or criticizing it.
Examples:
"They are concern trolling by pretending to be worried about free speech, but they just want to spread hate"; "Don't listen to the concern trolls, they don't really care."
Characteristic Use:
Used to subtly undermine and discredit opposing viewpoints under the guise of genuine concern.
Preemptive Accusation of -Ism/Phobia
Explanation:
A tactic of immediately labeling any dissent or questioning of woke ideology as a form of prejudice (racism, sexism, transphobia, etc.) before any actual argument is made.
Examples:
"That question is transphobic"; "You are just showing your racism by disagreeing."
Characteristic Use:
Used to silence dissent and preemptively discredit opposing viewpoints by associating them with bigotry.
Emotional Blackmail
Explanation:
Using emotional appeals and guilt to pressure individuals into conforming to woke ideology or taking specific actions, often by framing disagreement as morally reprehensible or harmful.
Examples:
"How can you be against this when people are suffering?"; "If you don't support this, you are a bad person."
Characteristic Use:
Used to manipulate behavior and enforce compliance through emotional pressure and the threat of social ostracization.
Seizing the Moral High Ground
Explanation:
Framing their position as inherently morally superior, often used to preemptively shut down criticism and imply opponents are immoral.
Examples:
"We are on the right side of history"; "Our cause is just and righteous."
Characteristic Use:
Used to shut down debate by positioning their viewpoint as morally unassailable and opponents as morally deficient.
Reinterpreting Definitions
Explanation:
Redefining common terms (like "racism" or "violence") to broaden their scope and include systemic or emotional aspects, often to expand the reach of "woke" ideology.
Examples:
"Racism is not just individual prejudice, it's systemic power"; "Violence can be emotional, not just physical."
Characteristic Use:
Used to expand the definition of negative concepts to include broader societal structures and emotional experiences, thereby widening the scope of "woke" critique.
Weaponizing Empathy
Explanation:
Using emotional appeals and narratives of suffering to manipulate and pressure others into agreement, often exploiting empathy for political ends.
Examples:
"Think of the children who are suffering"; "How can you not feel empathy for these victims?"
Characteristic Use:
Used to bypass logical arguments and manipulate emotions to enforce agreement and guilt-trip dissenters.
Demand for Unconditional Acceptance
Explanation:
Not just tolerance, but active validation and affirmation of specific identities or beliefs, with dissent framed as bigotry and intolerance.
Examples:
"You must affirm my identity"; "Tolerance is not enough, we demand affirmation."
Characteristic Use:
Used to enforce ideological conformity by requiring not just passive acceptance but active endorsement of specific viewpoints.
Presenting Opinions as Facts
Explanation:
Stating ideological positions as if they are objective truths, silencing alternative viewpoints by framing them as factually incorrect or ignorant.
Examples:
"It's a fact that gender is a social construct"; "Systemic racism is simply a fact."
Characteristic Use:
Used to shut down debate by presenting ideological claims as established truths, leaving no room for questioning or alternative interpretations.
Shifting Goalposts
Explanation:
Changing the criteria for success or progress mid-discussion to avoid admitting fault or acknowledging counterarguments, often used to maintain the appearance of always being "right."
Examples:
"Well, that policy didn't work, but now we need to focus on this other aspect"; "That's not the real issue, the real issue is..."
Characteristic Use:
Used to avoid accountability and maintain ideological purity by constantly changing the focus and criteria of evaluation.
DARVO (Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender)
Explanation:
A tactic often observed when individuals are called out for harmful behavior, where they deny the behavior, attack the accuser, and claim to be the real victim.
Examples:
"I never said that, you're twisting my words"; "You're attacking me unfairly"; "I'm the one being victimized by this witch hunt."
Characteristic Use:
Used to deflect accountability, silence critics, and portray themselves as victims of unfair persecution, even when confronted with evidence of harmful behavior.
Concern Bombing
Explanation:
Overwhelming opponents with a barrage of seemingly caring or concerned messages, often to exhaust them or make them feel guilty, while actually being manipulative.
Examples:
"Are you *sure* you're okay with that? It seems harmful"; "I'm just *concerned* about the impact of your words."
Characteristic Use:
Used to subtly undermine and guilt-trip opponents under the guise of genuine care and concern.
Love Bombing (Ideological)
Explanation:
Initially showering new recruits or converts with excessive praise, affirmation, and a sense of belonging to quickly indoctrinate them into the ideology, creating a strong emotional bond and discouraging dissent.
Examples:
"Welcome to the community! We are so happy to have you!"; "You are so insightful and brave for speaking out!"
Characteristic Use:
Used to quickly build loyalty and commitment to the ideology, making it harder for individuals to question or leave later.
Purity Spirals
Explanation:
A dynamic within groups where individuals compete to demonstrate their ideological purity and commitment, often leading to increasingly extreme and dogmatic positions, and the expulsion of those deemed insufficiently "pure."
Examples:
"Who is the most woke in the room?"; "We need to be even more radical to be truly effective."
Characteristic Use:
Used to drive ideological extremism and create a climate of fear and conformity within groups.
Splintering/Infighting
Explanation:
Groups fracturing and dividing due to ideological disagreements or purity tests, often leading to smaller, more extreme factions that are even less tolerant of dissent.
Examples:
"The movement splintered over ideological purity"; "Infighting is destroying our movement."
Characteristic Use:
A common consequence of ideological rigidity and purity spirals, weakening the overall movement and hindering broader appeal.
Moral Grandstanding
Explanation:
Publicly expressing moral outrage or taking performative actions primarily to impress others and elevate one's own social status, rather than out of genuine concern for the issue.
Examples:
"Look at me, I'm so outraged by this!"; "They are just grandstanding to get attention."
Characteristic Use:
Used to gain social capital and appear virtuous, often at the expense of genuine action or meaningful change.
Motte and Bailey Doctrine
Explanation:
Advancing a controversial or extreme position (the "bailey"), but when challenged, retreating to a more moderate and defensible position (the "motte"), then returning to the extreme position when the criticism subsides.
Examples:
Initially advocating for abolishing prisons, but when challenged, claiming to only want prison reform, then gradually returning to abolitionist rhetoric.
Characteristic Use:
Used to subtly push extreme ideas while avoiding direct confrontation and maintaining plausible deniability.
Just Asking Questions (JAQing Off)
Explanation:
Pretending to be innocently seeking information or clarification, while actually subtly promoting a biased or harmful viewpoint through leading questions and insinuation.
Examples:
"I'm just asking questions, but isn't it possible that...?"; "I'm just curious, but what about...?"
Characteristic Use:
Used to spread misinformation or sow doubt without directly stating controversial opinions, maintaining a facade of innocent inquiry.
Moving the Overton Window
Explanation:
Gradually shifting public discourse and what is considered acceptable or mainstream by repeatedly advocating for increasingly extreme positions, making previously radical ideas seem more moderate over time.
Examples:
Starting with calls for diversity and inclusion, then gradually shifting to demands for equity and reparations.
Characteristic Use:
A long-term strategy to normalize radical ideas by incrementally pushing the boundaries of acceptable discourse.
Flooding the Zone with Shit
Explanation:
Overwhelming public discourse with a large volume of misinformation, propaganda, and distractions, making it difficult to discern truth from falsehood and eroding trust in reliable sources of information.
Examples:
Spreading countless articles and social media posts promoting a specific ideology, regardless of factual accuracy.
Characteristic Use:
Used to confuse and disorient the public, making it harder to resist or critique the ideology being promoted.
Astroturfing
Explanation:
Creating the false impression of widespread grassroots support for a particular viewpoint or movement, often through coordinated online campaigns or fake accounts, to manipulate public opinion.
Examples:
Using bot networks to amplify social media posts and create the illusion of popular support.
Characteristic Use:
Used to artificially inflate the perceived popularity and legitimacy of a movement or ideology.
Grooming (Ideological)
Explanation:
Gradually indoctrinating individuals, especially young or vulnerable people, into a specific ideology or belief system, often through manipulative tactics and emotional appeals, creating dependency and discouraging critical thinking.
Examples:
Targeting children with age-inappropriate or ideologically biased content in schools or online.
Characteristic Use:
Used to recruit and indoctrinate new followers, especially from younger generations, ensuring the long-term propagation of the ideology.
Echo Chamber Creation/Maintenance
Explanation:
Actively creating and reinforcing online and offline spaces where only certain viewpoints are allowed or amplified, suppressing dissenting opinions and limiting exposure to diverse perspectives, strengthening groupthink and ideological conformity.
Examples:
Moderating online forums to remove dissenting voices and promote ideological homogeneity.
Characteristic Use:
Used to insulate adherents from criticism and reinforce their beliefs, creating a self-contained and self-validating ideological bubble.
Thought-Terminating Cliches
Explanation:
Using short, simplistic slogans or phrases that shut down critical thinking and discourage further inquiry or debate, often appealing to emotions rather than logic.
Examples:
"Love is Love"; "Black Lives Matter"; "Silence is Violence."
Characteristic Use:
Used to simplify complex issues and discourage nuanced discussion, promoting emotional responses over rational analysis.
Loaded Language/Buzzwords
Explanation:
Using emotionally charged words or phrases that are designed to evoke strong positive or negative reactions, often used to manipulate emotions and bypass rational thought.
Examples:
"Justice," "Equality," "Oppression," "Hate," "Bigotry."
Characteristic Use:
Used to frame issues in emotionally charged terms, making it harder to engage in objective analysis and critical thinking.
Gaslighting the Opposition (Societal Scale)
Explanation:
Systematically denying or distorting the reality of opposing viewpoints or concerns on a societal scale, making those who disagree feel confused, isolated, or even mentally unstable, eroding their confidence in their own perceptions and judgments.
Examples:
Dismissing concerns about free speech as "right-wing talking points" or labeling any criticism of "woke" ideology as "bigotry."
Characteristic Use:
Used to undermine the credibility and legitimacy of opposing viewpoints on a broad scale, making it harder for dissent to gain traction or be taken seriously.

IV. Reasoning and Problem-Solving Flaws

Goal-Oriented Tunnel Vision
Explanation:
Focusing on desired outcomes without any consideration for the system or practicality of how to achieve that goal. Often, unintended consequences are completely ignored, because the focus is solely on the "end goal."
Examples:
"We need to make sure all people have equal outcomes, and anything is justified in pursuit of that goal."
Characteristic Use:
The justification of any means to achieve a "just" outcome, regardless of real-world consequences.
Ignoring Second Order Effects
Explanation:
The inability to understand that all actions will generate a reaction, and that problems will often get worse if not approached with care and proper understanding. This often leads to policies that create new problems, while trying to solve old ones.
Examples:
"We must implement this new law right now, with no planning on how it will impact other groups of people, or other areas of society."
Characteristic Use:
A tendency to implement new policies or ideas without considering all the potential impacts.
Incentive Blindness
Explanation:
Overlooking or ignoring how incentives drive behavior and affect the effectiveness of a system. Often, people are assumed to act in certain ways, even if they have no real incentive to do so.
Examples:
"We will fix poverty if we just pay people more, without changing any of their behavior."
Characteristic Use:
The assumption that people will act in certain ways simply because it's the "right thing to do," without considering their individual incentives.
Ignoring Self-Interest
Explanation:
The assumption that people will act in a certain way just because it is the "right thing to do," without understanding how their incentives are pushing them in another direction.
Examples:
"We should pay people less because it's the right thing to do, and everyone will agree."
Characteristic Use:
A naive belief that all individuals will sacrifice for "the greater good" without considering human nature.
Reductionist Thinking
Explanation:
Reducing complex systems down to a single cause or issue, ignoring the larger context.
Examples:
"All problems can be traced to racism"; "Capitalism is the root of all evil, and once we get rid of it, everything will be better."
Characteristic Use:
A tendency to oversimplify complex problems to fit a pre-existing narrative.
Lack of Holistic Thinking
Explanation:
Focusing on one part of a system, without understanding how that change might affect the system as a whole.
Examples:
"We will reduce crime by reducing the police force, and nothing bad will happen."
Characteristic Use:
A tendency to focus on individual elements without considering their effects on the entire system.
Ignoring Feedback Loops
Explanation:
The inability to understand that all actions will generate a reaction, and that problems will often get worse if not approached with care and proper understanding.
Examples:
"We can get rid of this policy, and nothing bad will happen."
Characteristic Use:
The inability to acknowledge how changes can cause unintended and often negative consequences.
Zero-Sum Thinking
Explanation:
The belief that in any interaction or system, if one person or group gains, another person or group must lose. This ignores the fact that most interactions can be beneficial to all parties.
Examples:
"If we give more resources to marginalized groups, that means less resources for everyone else." "If this group is succeeding, that means it must be at the expense of another group."
Characteristic Use:
The idea that society is a zero-sum game and that resources need to be redistributed to create a fair system.
Presentism
Explanation:
Judging past actions based on present day values and standards, without accounting for the context of the times.
Examples:
"That historical figure was evil because they did not align with today's ideas." "This historical event is bad because it goes against modern morality."
Characteristic Use:
A tendency to judge historical figures and actions by today's moral standards, instead of considering the context of the past.
Appeal to Emotion
Explanation:
Using emotions rather than logic, to make a point.
Examples:
"How can you stand against the vulnerable people?"; "If you do not agree with us, then you must be evil."
Characteristic Use:
A reliance on emotional arguments rather than rational ones, and appealing to people's desire to do good.
Moral Licensing
Explanation:
A tendency to think that because you have done something "good" or "moral" in the past, that you are free to act less morally in the future.
Examples:
"I donate to charity, so that makes up for some of my bad behavior"; "I have stood up for my views, so now I am allowed to harass this group of people."
Characteristic Use:
A tendency to believe that because they support marginalized groups, that they can get away with bad behavior.
Lack of Intellectual Honesty
Explanation:
Refusing to acknowledge legitimate criticisms and questions.
Examples:
"That's a bigoted viewpoint and you are not allowed to say that here"; "You cannot ask that question, it is too harmful."
Characteristic Use:
Shutting down dissent, and avoiding hard conversations in order to protect the integrity of the ideology.
Confirmation Bias
Explanation:
Only accepting ideas and evidence that confirms their existing beliefs, and rejecting anything that goes against their current understanding of the world.
Examples:
"If I see one news article that supports my views, then I will accept that as fact, even if it goes against evidence"; "This person said something that I agreed with, so I will accept everything else they say."
Characteristic Use:
A tendency to accept all information that confirms their existing viewpoint, while rejecting anything that might challenge that worldview.
The Halo Effect
Explanation:
If someone agrees with you on one thing, then you assume they are good in all areas.
Examples:
"That person is woke, therefore, they are correct about everything"; "That person is good, because they support this cause that I also support."
Characteristic Use:
A tendency to judge people's overall character and trustworthiness based on a single political belief.
Straw Man Argument
Explanation:
Misrepresenting an opponent's argument to make it easier to attack.
Examples:
"They say they want lower taxes, but what they really want is to starve poor people."
Characteristic Use:
Used to mischaracterize opposing viewpoints and make them seem extreme or unreasonable.
Ad Hominem Fallacy
Explanation:
Attacking the person making an argument, rather than addressing the argument itself.
Examples:
"You can't trust their opinion on climate change, they are a known bigot."
Characteristic Use:
Used to dismiss arguments based on the perceived flaws of the person making them, rather than the merit of the argument itself.
Appeal to Authority
Explanation:
Claiming something is true simply because an authority figure or expert said so, without sufficient evidence or critical evaluation.
Examples:
"This famous professor says this is true, so it must be true."
Characteristic Use:
Used to lend undue weight to claims, even if the "authority" is not an expert in the relevant field or if there is no consensus on the issue.
False Cause Fallacy (Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc)
Explanation:
Assuming that because two events are correlated or happen in sequence, one caused the other.
Examples:
"Since wokeness became popular, crime has increased, therefore wokeness causes crime."
Characteristic Use:
Used to blame unrelated phenomena on "woke" ideology or social justice movements.
Bandwagon Effect
Explanation:
Believing something is true or right simply because many other people believe it.
Examples:
"Everyone knows that systemic racism is everywhere, so it must be true."
Characteristic Use:
Used to create a sense of inevitability or overwhelming consensus around certain ideas.
Slippery Slope Fallacy
Explanation:
Arguing that an initial action will inevitably lead to a series of increasingly negative consequences, without sufficient evidence.
Examples:
"If we allow pronouns in email signatures, it will lead to the destruction of society."
Characteristic Use:
Used to exaggerate the potential negative consequences of social changes or progressive policies.
Anecdotal Evidence Fallacy
Explanation:
Using personal stories or isolated examples as evidence to support a broad claim, while ignoring statistical data or broader trends.
Examples:
"I know one person who was harmed by cancel culture, therefore cancel culture is a huge problem."
Characteristic Use:
Used to create emotional appeals and generalizations based on limited or unrepresentative examples.
Hasty Generalization
Explanation:
Drawing a broad conclusion based on limited or insufficient evidence.
Examples:
"I met a woke person who was rude, therefore all woke people are rude."
Characteristic Use:
Used to stereotype and demonize entire groups of people associated with "woke" ideology.
Appeal to Ignorance
Explanation:
Arguing that something is true because it has not been proven false, or false because it has not been proven true.
Examples:
"No one has proven systemic racism doesn't exist, therefore it must exist."
Characteristic Use:
Used to shift the burden of proof and make unsubstantiated claims seem plausible.
Groupthink
Explanation:
A psychological phenomenon that occurs within a group of people in which the desire for harmony or conformity in the group results in irrational or dysfunctional decision-making.
Examples:
"Everyone in our woke group agrees on this, so it must be correct."
Characteristic Use:
Can explain the lack of critical thinking and dissenting opinions within groups that strongly adhere to "woke" ideology.
Appeal to Incredulity Fallacy
Explanation:
Dismissing an argument or viewpoint simply because it seems unbelievable or counterintuitive to one's own worldview, often used to reject criticisms of woke ideology.
Examples:
"I just can't believe that could be true, therefore it isn't"; "That's too unbelievable to be real."
Characteristic Use:
Used to shut down arguments that challenge pre-existing beliefs by framing them as inherently unbelievable or absurd.
Essentialism/Stereotyping
Explanation:
Ironically, while critiquing stereotyping, "woke" discourse can sometimes fall into essentializing entire groups, ironically mirroring the very behavior they criticize.
Examples:
"All white people are inherently privileged"; "All BIPOC share the same experience and think alike."
Characteristic Use:
Used to make broad, sweeping generalizations about entire groups of people based on identity categories, undermining individual differences and complexity.
Emotional Reasoning
Explanation:
Treating feelings as facts and making arguments based primarily on emotional appeals rather than logic or evidence, prioritizing emotional validation over rational analysis.
Examples:
"I feel that this is racist, therefore it is racist"; "My feelings are valid, and that's all that matters."
Characteristic Use:
Used to bypass logical argumentation and elevate subjective feelings as the ultimate arbiter of truth and morality.
Motivated Reasoning
Explanation:
Reasoning in a way that primarily serves to confirm pre-existing beliefs or desired conclusions, rather than seeking objective truth, prioritizing ideological confirmation over factual accuracy.
Examples:
"I will interpret this data to fit my woke worldview"; "I will only accept evidence that supports my pre-conceived notions."
Characteristic Use:
Used to selectively interpret information and prioritize ideological alignment over objective analysis and intellectual honesty.
Lack of Proportionality
Explanation:
Responding to minor transgressions with extreme outrage or punishment, disproportionate to the actual harm caused, exhibiting an inability to distinguish between minor offenses and serious harms.
Examples:
"Forgetting someone's pronouns is an act of violence"; "A slightly insensitive joke warrants complete social ostracization."
Characteristic Use:
Used to justify excessive and disproportionate reactions to minor offenses, creating a climate of fear and over-sensitivity.
The Availability Heuristic
Explanation:
Over-relying on readily available or memorable examples when making judgments or evaluating the frequency of events, leading to distorted perceptions of reality.
Examples:
"I saw a video of cancel culture ruining someone's life, so cancel culture must be a widespread and devastating problem."
Characteristic Use:
Can lead to exaggerating the prevalence or severity of issues based on easily accessible anecdotes or media portrayals, rather than statistical data.
Confirmation Bias in Information Seeking
Explanation:
Actively seeking out information that confirms pre-existing beliefs and avoiding information that challenges them, reinforcing echo chambers and limiting exposure to diverse perspectives.
Examples:
"I only read news sources that align with my woke worldview"; "I ignore any articles that criticize social justice movements."
Characteristic Use:
Contributes to ideological entrenchment and resistance to changing one's mind, even in the face of contradictory evidence.
Belief Perseverance
Explanation:
Continuing to hold onto beliefs even after they have been discredited or proven false, resisting evidence that contradicts one's worldview and maintaining ideological rigidity.
Examples:
"Even though studies show this policy doesn't work, I still believe it's the right thing to do"; "I will ignore any evidence that challenges my woke beliefs."
Characteristic Use:
Explains the persistence of ideological beliefs even in the face of factual refutation, leading to resistance to correction or compromise.
The Dunning-Kruger Effect (in Social Justice Domains)
Explanation:
Individuals with limited knowledge or expertise in social justice issues overestimating their understanding and competence, while lacking self-awareness of their own ignorance or biases.
Examples:
"I just became woke last week, and now I'm an expert on systemic oppression"; "I know more about racism than anyone else, even though I just started learning about it."
Characteristic Use:
Can lead to overconfidence and dogmatism in expressing opinions on complex social issues, despite a lack of deep understanding or critical self-reflection.
The Backfire Effect
Explanation:
When presented with evidence that contradicts their beliefs, individuals may not only fail to change their minds, but may actually strengthen their original beliefs in response, becoming even more entrenched in their ideology.
Examples:
"When someone shows me data that contradicts my woke beliefs, I just become even more convinced that I'm right"; "Challenging my worldview only makes me double down on my beliefs."
Characteristic Use:
Explains why attempts to debunk or critique "woke" ideology can sometimes backfire and further solidify adherents' commitment to their beliefs.
Confirmation Bias in Memory
Explanation:
Selectively remembering information that confirms pre-existing beliefs and forgetting or distorting information that contradicts them, shaping memories to align with one's worldview.
Examples:
"I tend to remember examples that support my woke beliefs and forget those that challenge them"; "My memories are biased to fit my ideology."
Characteristic Use:
Contributes to a biased and selective recollection of past events and experiences, reinforcing existing ideological perspectives and limiting openness to alternative interpretations.
The Sunk Cost Fallacy (in Ideological Commitment)
Explanation:
Continuing to invest in a particular ideology or belief system, even when it becomes clear that it is flawed or harmful, because of the time, effort, or social capital already invested, leading to a reluctance to admit mistakes or change course.
Examples:
"I've invested so much time and energy into being woke, I can't admit that it might be wrong"; "I'm too invested in this ideology to back down now."
Characteristic Use:
Explains the reluctance of individuals to abandon or question "woke" ideology, even when faced with evidence of its negative consequences or internal contradictions.
The Illusion of Explanatory Depth
Explanation:
Individuals believing they understand complex issues more deeply than they actually do, often revealed when they are asked to explain the details of their understanding, exposing a superficial grasp of the subject matter.
Examples:
"I think I understand systemic racism, but when asked to explain it in detail, I realize I don't really know"; "I have a superficial understanding of these complex issues, but I overestimate my knowledge."
Characteristic Use:
Can contribute to overconfidence in expressing opinions on complex social issues, without possessing a genuine or nuanced understanding of the underlying dynamics.
The Bias Blind Spot
Explanation:
Recognizing that biases exist and affect others' judgments, while failing to recognize the influence of biases on one's own judgments, leading to an overconfidence in one's own objectivity and rationality.
Examples:
"I know that other people are biased, but I believe I am objective and unbiased"; "I can see biases in others, but not in myself."
Characteristic Use:
Contributes to a lack of self-awareness and an inability to recognize one's own ideological biases, reinforcing dogmatism and limiting openness to self-correction.
Affect Heuristic
Explanation:
Making judgments and decisions based on immediate emotional reactions (affect) rather than rational analysis, allowing feelings to override logical reasoning and evidence-based evaluation.
Examples:
"I just *feel* that this is right, so I'm going to go with my gut"; "My emotions tell me this is true, so I don't need to think about it logically."
Characteristic Use:
Prioritizing emotional responses over rational thought, leading to decisions and judgments that are driven by feelings rather than evidence or logic.
The Dunning-Kruger Effect (in Logical Reasoning)
Explanation:
Individuals with low logical reasoning abilities often overestimating their logical competence, while lacking self-awareness of their own reasoning flaws and cognitive biases, contributing to poor decision-making and flawed arguments.
Examples:
"I think I'm a very logical person, but I often fall prey to logical fallacies"; "I overestimate my own reasoning abilities and don't realize how flawed my logic is."
Characteristic Use:
Can lead to using flawed logic and making poor arguments while being unaware of one's own reasoning deficits, reinforcing flawed thinking patterns.
The Narrative Fallacy
Explanation:
The tendency to construct narratives or stories to make sense of events or information, even when those narratives are oversimplified, misleading, or impose artificial order on complex or random phenomena, leading to a distorted understanding of reality.
Examples:
"I created a simple narrative to explain systemic racism, even though it's much more complex than that"; "I oversimplify complex issues by fitting them into neat stories."
Characteristic Use:
Can lead to oversimplified and inaccurate understandings of social issues, based on compelling but ultimately flawed narratives rather than nuanced analysis.
The Planning Fallacy
Explanation:
Consistently underestimating the time, resources, and effort required to complete tasks or implement plans, while overestimating one's own abilities and control over future events, leading to unrealistic expectations and failed projects.
Examples:
"We thought we could dismantle systemic oppression quickly and easily, but it's taking much longer than we expected"; "We underestimate the complexity and difficulty of achieving our social justice goals."
Characteristic Use:
Can lead to unrealistic expectations and disappointment in social justice activism, as well as a failure to adequately plan for the long-term challenges of systemic change.
The Outcome Bias
Explanation:
Evaluating the quality of a decision based solely on its outcome, rather than the process or information available at the time the decision was made, judging past choices based on present knowledge, overlooking the uncertainty and context of the decision-making process.
Examples:
"Since this social justice initiative failed, it must have been a bad idea from the start"; "We judge past actions based on current outcomes, rather than the information available at the time."
Characteristic Use:
Can lead to unfair criticism of past actions or decisions, without acknowledging the uncertainty and limited information that decision-makers faced at the time.
The Survivorship Bias
Explanation:
Concentrating on successful examples or "survivors" while ignoring failures or non-survivors, leading to distorted conclusions and inaccurate assessments of risk or effectiveness, overlooking the broader population and focusing only on visible successes.
Examples:
"We only hear about successful woke activists, but we ignore the many who have been harmed or disillusioned"; "We focus on successful examples of social justice movements, but not the many failures."
Characteristic Use:
Can lead to an overly optimistic or biased view of the effectiveness of "woke" ideology or social justice movements, by focusing only on visible successes and ignoring less visible failures or negative consequences.
The Availability Cascade
Explanation:
A self-reinforcing process where a collective belief gains increasing plausibility through its increasing repetition in public discourse (often in the media), regardless of its factual basis, leading to widespread acceptance of ideas based on repetition rather than evidence.
Examples:
"The more we hear about systemic racism in the media, the more people believe it, even if the evidence is weak"; "Repeatedly hearing about 'white privilege' makes it seem more real, even without strong evidence."
Characteristic Use:
Can explain the rapid spread and acceptance of certain "woke" ideas, even when they are not well-supported by evidence, due to their constant repetition and amplification in media and social discourse.
The Representativeness Heuristic
Explanation:
Judging the probability of an event or the characteristics of a person based on how similar they are to a prototype or stereotype, rather than considering base rates or statistical probabilities, leading to biased judgments and inaccurate predictions.
Examples:
"This person looks like a 'woke' activist, so they must be"; "That event fits my stereotype of systemic oppression, so it must be an example of it."
Characteristic Use:
Can lead to stereotyping and biased judgments of individuals and events based on superficial similarities to pre-conceived categories or stereotypes associated with "woke" ideology.
The Anchoring Bias
Explanation:
Over-relying on the first piece of information received (the "anchor") when making judgments or decisions, even when that information is irrelevant or misleading, biasing subsequent judgments towards the initial anchor.
Examples:
"Once we hear a powerful narrative about systemic racism, it becomes the anchor for all our thinking about race"; "Our initial exposure to 'woke' ideology biases our subsequent understanding."
Characteristic Use:
Can lead to biased and inflexible thinking, as initial exposure to certain ideas or narratives can disproportionately influence subsequent judgments and interpretations.
The Framing Effect
Explanation:
Drawing different conclusions from the same information depending on how it is presented or "framed," with subtle changes in wording or emphasis significantly influencing judgments and decisions, even when the underlying facts remain the same.
Examples:
"Framing an issue as 'social justice' makes it more appealing than framing it as 'radical change'"; "Presenting the same policy in different ways can elicit very different responses."
Characteristic Use:
Used to manipulate public opinion and influence decision-making by strategically framing issues in a way that elicits desired emotional responses or biases.
The Confirmation Trap
Explanation:
Actively seeking out and interpreting information in a way that confirms pre-existing beliefs, while ignoring or downplaying contradictory information, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of confirmation bias and ideological entrenchment.
Examples:
"We actively seek out information that supports our woke worldview and dismiss anything that challenges it"; "We fall into a trap of only confirming what we already believe."
Characteristic Use:
Reinforces ideological echo chambers and makes it increasingly difficult to challenge or change one's beliefs, leading to dogmatism and resistance to critical self-reflection.
The Identifiable Victim Effect
Explanation:
Being more emotionally moved and willing to help identifiable individuals than anonymous or statistical victims, even when the scale of suffering is greater for the latter, prioritizing individual stories over statistical realities in eliciting empathy and action.
Examples:
"We are more moved by individual stories of injustice than by statistics about systemic inequality"; "Personal narratives of victimhood are more powerful than abstract data."
Characteristic Use:
Used to emotionally manipulate and mobilize support for social justice causes by focusing on individual stories of suffering, even when those stories may not be representative of broader trends or systemic issues.
