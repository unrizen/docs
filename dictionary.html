This document provides a definitive analysis of terms, concepts, patterns, and tactics commonly recognized as "wokisms" and left-wing propaganda. I. Language and Terminology

Allyship
Explanation:
The act of supporting marginalized groups, often with a focus on performative actions intended to signal virtue rather than enact meaningful change.
Examples:
"Posting a black square isn't true allyship"; "They claim to be an ally, but their actions say otherwise."
Characteristic Use:
Allyship functions as a tool to criticize others and elevate the moral standing of the "ally," often without requiring concrete action.
Risk:
The focus on "performative allyship" can distract from genuine efforts to address systemic issues and foster a culture of cynicism and moral posturing.
BIPOC
Explanation:
An acronym referring to Black, Indigenous, and People of Color, used to group diverse communities together, often in opposition to "white people."
Examples:
"We need to center BIPOC voices"; "This policy will disproportionately affect BIPOC communities."
Characteristic Use:
"BIPOC" essentializes diverse communities, presenting them as a unified bloc and obscuring important distinctions and individual identities.
Risk:
The use of "BIPOC" can oversimplify complex social realities, erase the unique experiences of different groups, and exacerbate racial divisions by framing society in terms of a binary opposition.
Cancel Culture
Explanation:
The practice of publicly withdrawing support for someone after an offense, frequently characterized by excessive harshness, intolerance, and suppression of free speech.
Examples:
"He was canceled for a tweet from years ago"; "Cancel culture is a threat to open discourse."
Characteristic Use:
Cancel culture functions as a tool to enforce ideological conformity within woke ideology, punishing dissent and discouraging open dialogue.
Risk:
Cancel culture poses a significant threat to free speech and open debate, fostering a climate of fear and self-censorship, and undermining principles of due process and forgiveness.
Centering
Explanation:
The act of intentionally focusing on the needs and experiences of specific marginalized groups, often to the exclusion of other perspectives or concerns.
Examples:
"We need to center trans voices in this conversation"; "Stop centering whiteness."
Characteristic Use:
Centering is used to elevate certain voices while marginalizing or suppressing others, creating a hierarchy of perspectives based on identity.
Risk:
The practice of centering can lead to exclusionary discourse, where certain viewpoints are deemed inherently more valuable than others based on group identity, undermining the principle of equal consideration and open debate.

Explanation:

Risk:

Cisgender
Explanation:

Examples:
"As a cisgender person, I have privilege"; "Cisgender people need to listen to trans experiences."
Characteristic Use:
"Cisgender" functions as a label to assign privilege based solely on gender identity, often used to invalidate perspectives and experiences.
Risk:
The term can be used to create division and resentment, assigning group-based guilt and hindering constructive dialogue by framing individuals primarily through the lens of identity-based privilege.
Cultural Appropriation
Explanation:
The act of taking elements of a culture by a dominant group without permission, understanding, or respect, often for profit or in a way that decontextualizes or trivializes the original meaning.
Examples:
"That singer's hairstyle is cultural appropriation"; "That company is guilty of cultural appropriation for selling that item."
Characteristic Use:
The concept of "cultural appropriation" is used to restrict free expression and creativity, policing cultural exchange and innovation.
Risk:
Overly broad application of "cultural appropriation" can stifle artistic expression, limit cross-cultural understanding, and create unnecessary divisions by discouraging cultural exchange and borrowing.

Explanation:

Examples:
"We need to decolonize our curriculum"; "Decolonize your mind."
Characteristic Use:
"Decolonize" functions as a vague but powerful call for radical change and the dismantling of existing institutions, without clear or practical alternatives.
Risk:
The term can be used to justify the destruction of valuable traditions and institutions without thoughtful consideration of consequences, potentially leading to societal instability and the loss of accumulated knowledge.

Characteristic Use:
The concept of "deadnaming" is used to police speech and behavior, punishing individuals for using names or pronouns that do not conform to current gender ideology.
Risk:
Overly strict enforcement of pronoun and name usage can stifle free expression, create a climate of fear around unintentional missteps, and prioritize language control over genuine respect and understanding.

Risk:
Subjective definitions of "harm" can be weaponized to suppress speech, stifle debate, and create an overly sensitive environment where legitimate discourse is curtailed in the name of emotional safety.

"Hate speech" is used to shut down debate and criticism, labeling dissenting opinions as inherently hateful and therefore illegitimate.
Risk:
Broad and subjective definitions of "hate speech" undermine free speech principles, leading to censorship of legitimate viewpoints and creating an environment where open discussion of sensitive topics is stifled.

Focusing solely on historical exclusion can lead to present-day discrimination, overlooking individual merit and perpetuating group-based preferences that undermine equality and fairness.
