The Skeptic's Dictionary of Right-Wing Terms and Tactics

This document provides a definitive analysis of terms, concepts, patterns, and tactics commonly recognized as right-wing propaganda. It serves as an essential guide to understanding the underlying mechanisms and potential negative consequences associated with these elements. This dictionary empowers critical thinking by illuminating the characteristic uses and inherent risks of these terms and tactics in contemporary discourse.

I. Language and Terminology

"Witch Hunt"
Explanation:
Describing legitimate investigations or criticisms as baseless persecutions, used to deflect accountability and rally support by portraying oneself as a victim.
Examples:
"This impeachment is a witch hunt!"; "The media is conducting a witch hunt against conservatives."
Characteristic Use:
"Witch hunt" is used to delegitimize scrutiny and frame any form of accountability as an unjust attack, regardless of evidence.
Risk:
The term trivializes actual historical injustices and undermines the importance of legitimate oversight and accountability, fostering a culture of impunity.
"Fake News"
Explanation:
Labeling factual reporting that is critical or unfavorable as fabricated, used to discredit legitimate journalism and control narratives by dismissing inconvenient truths.
Examples:
"Don't believe the fake news media!"; "That article is just fake news designed to attack us."
Characteristic Use:
"Fake news" is weaponized to undermine trust in established media and create an environment where only ideologically aligned sources are deemed credible.
Risk:
The term erodes trust in credible sources of information, promotes misinformation, and makes it difficult for the public to discern fact from fiction, undermining informed discourse.
"Deep State"
Explanation:
A conspiratorial term suggesting a hidden cabal within government institutions is secretly controlling events, used to foster distrust in government and justify radical actions against established systems.
Examples:
"The deep state is working against the President"; "The bureaucracy is controlled by the deep state."
Characteristic Use:
"Deep state" promotes paranoia and undermines faith in democratic institutions and processes, fostering a climate of distrust and suspicion.
Risk:
The concept erodes trust in legitimate government functions, encourages conspiracy thinking, and can justify anti-democratic actions in the name of fighting a phantom enemy.
"Globalist"
Explanation:
A term often used with anti-Semitic undertones, suggesting a shadowy elite prioritizing global interests over national ones, used to scapegoat and demonize perceived opponents of nationalism.
Examples:
"The globalists are destroying national sovereignty"; "We need to fight the globalist agenda."
Characteristic Use:
"Globalist" functions as a dog whistle, often targeting Jewish people and other minorities, promoting xenophobia and nationalist fervor.
Risk:
The term fuels xenophobia, anti-Semitism, and isolationism, undermining international cooperation and fostering division and prejudice.
"Socialist" (as a pejorative)
Explanation:
Broadly labeling any left-leaning policy or individual as "socialist" to evoke fear and historical associations with authoritarian regimes, used to discredit progressive ideas regardless of their actual nature.
Examples:
"Democrats are socialists!"; "That policy is socialist and will destroy America."
Characteristic Use:
"Socialist" is used as a scare tactic to demonize any policy perceived as left-of-center, regardless of its democratic nature or practical merits.
Risk:
The term stifles nuanced debate about economic and social policies, promotes ideological polarization, and misrepresents democratic socialist ideas as inherently authoritarian.
"Liberal Elite"
Explanation:
A term used to portray those with progressive views as out-of-touch, condescending, and privileged, despite often representing diverse socioeconomic backgrounds, used to stoke resentment and division.
Examples:
"The liberal elite doesn't understand real America"; "Hollywood and universities are controlled by the liberal elite."
Characteristic Use:
"Liberal elite" fosters an "us vs. them" mentality, framing progressive viewpoints as inherently disconnected from the concerns of ordinary people.
Risk:
The term promotes anti-intellectualism, discourages empathy and understanding across ideological divides, and simplifies complex social and political landscapes into simplistic binaries.
"Culture Wars"
Explanation:
Framing social and political issues as a battle between opposing cultural values, often exaggerating differences and demonizing opposing viewpoints, used to mobilize base and create division.
Examples:
"We are in a culture war for the soul of America"; "The left is waging a culture war against traditional values."
Characteristic Use:
"Culture wars" rhetoric is used to inflame passions, simplify complex social issues into moralistic battles, and rally political support through fear and resentment.
Risk:
The concept exacerbates social divisions, undermines compromise and nuanced debate, and promotes a climate of hostility and intolerance towards differing perspectives.
"Freedom Fries" / "Freedom Toast"
Explanation:
Changing names of everyday items to reflect nationalistic sentiment, often during times of international tension, used for performative patriotism and to demonize foreign entities.
Examples:
"Let's order some Freedom Fries!"; "Serve Freedom Toast at your patriotic breakfast."
Characteristic Use:
These terms are used for simplistic displays of nationalism, often coupled with xenophobia and a rejection of foreign cultures or viewpoints.
Risk:
The terms trivialize complex international relations, encourage simplistic "us vs. them" thinking, and promote jingoism over genuine understanding of global issues.
"Critical Race Theory" (as a boogeyman)
Explanation:
Misrepresenting a complex academic framework as a radical ideology that teaches people to hate America and white people, used to stoke racial anxieties and mobilize opposition to racial justice initiatives.
Examples:
"Critical Race Theory is destroying our schools!"; "CRT is anti-white and un-American."
Characteristic Use:
"Critical Race Theory" is weaponized to create fear and opposition to discussions about systemic racism and efforts to promote racial equity.
Risk:
The term stifles honest conversations about race and history, promotes misinformation about academic disciplines, and undermines efforts to address racial inequality and injustice.
"Cancel Culture" (Right-Wing Usage)
Explanation:
Using the term "cancel culture" to describe any negative consequences for expressing controversial or offensive views, often ignoring the context and impact of the speech, used to portray oneself as a victim of censorship.
Examples:
"I was canceled for saying something politically incorrect!"; "Right-wing voices are being silenced by cancel culture."
Characteristic Use:
"Cancel culture" is used to deflect accountability for harmful speech and frame any criticism as an attack on free speech, regardless of the nature of the speech.
Risk:
The term trivializes genuine instances of censorship and undermines the importance of social consequences for harmful or hateful speech, promoting a culture of irresponsibility.
"Illegals" (vs. "Undocumented Immigrants")
Explanation:
Using dehumanizing language to refer to undocumented immigrants, framing them as criminals rather than individuals in complex situations, used to justify harsh immigration policies.
Examples:
"We need to deport all the illegals!"; "Illegals are a drain on our resources."
Characteristic Use:
"Illegals" is used to dehumanize immigrants, strip them of their individual humanity, and create a climate of fear and hostility towards immigrant communities.
Risk:
The term promotes xenophobia, dehumanization, and discriminatory policies, undermining empathy and understanding towards immigrants and fostering a climate of prejudice.
"Radical Left"
Explanation:
Broadly labeling mainstream liberal or progressive viewpoints as "radical" to create fear and alarm, used to delegitimize any policy outside of a narrow conservative spectrum.
Examples:
"The radical left is taking over the country!"; "We must stop the radical left agenda."
Characteristic Use:
"Radical left" is used to demonize and marginalize mainstream political opponents, framing them as extreme and dangerous regardless of their actual policy positions.
Risk:
The term polarizes political discourse, discourages compromise and cooperation, and misrepresents mainstream progressive ideas as extremist, undermining democratic processes.
"Open Borders" (Straw Man)
Explanation:
Misrepresenting nuanced immigration reform proposals as advocating for completely open borders with no controls, used to create fear and opposition to any immigration reform.
Examples:
"Democrats want open borders and no security!"; "Open borders will destroy our country."
Characteristic Use:
"Open borders" straw man is used to derail discussions about immigration reform and create fear around any changes to current policies, regardless of their actual details.
Risk:
The term prevents productive conversation about immigration policy, relies on fear-mongering and misinformation, and demonizes those advocating for more humane or reformed immigration systems.
"Build the Wall"
Explanation:
A simplistic slogan promising a physical barrier as a complete solution to complex immigration issues, used to rally support through fear and xenophobia, ignoring practicalities and ethical considerations.
Examples:
"Build the wall and keep them out!"; "The wall will solve our immigration problems."
Characteristic Use:
"Build the wall" is used to oversimplify complex immigration issues and promote a nationalist, exclusionary vision of border security.
Risk:
The slogan promotes xenophobia, ignores the complexities of immigration, and offers a false sense of security while diverting resources from more effective and humane solutions.
"Drain the Swamp"
Explanation:
A vague term promising to remove corruption from Washington, D.C., used to appeal to populist sentiment and justify radical actions against established institutions, often without clear or consistent definitions of "the swamp."
Examples:
"We need to drain the swamp in Washington!"; "The swamp is full of corrupt politicians and bureaucrats."
Characteristic Use:
"Drain the swamp" is used to foster distrust in government and justify sweeping changes, often without specific plans or accountability.
Risk:
The term promotes cynicism about government, can justify authoritarian tendencies, and often serves as a cover for replacing one form of self-serving power with another.
"Christian Nation" Rhetoric
Explanation:
Promoting the idea that the United States should be governed by Christian principles and values, often to the exclusion of other faiths or secular viewpoints, used to mobilize religious voters and undermine separation of church and state.
Examples:
"America is a Christian nation!"; "We need to restore Christian values to our government."
Characteristic Use:
"Christian nation" rhetoric is used to appeal to religious nationalism and create a sense of religious superiority and entitlement in the public sphere.
Risk:
The rhetoric undermines religious pluralism, promotes religious discrimination, and threatens the separation of church and state, fundamental principles of a democratic society.
"Woke" (Right-Wing Usage)
Explanation:
Originally meaning awareness of social injustice, now used pejoratively to broadly dismiss any progressive social or cultural idea as absurd, overly sensitive, or harmful, used to shut down discussions about social justice.
Examples:
"That company is going woke!"; "Don't be so woke."
Characteristic Use:
"Woke" functions as a catch-all insult to dismiss and ridicule any progressive idea, regardless of its merit or substance, hindering constructive dialogue.
Risk:
The term trivializes legitimate concerns about social justice, promotes anti-intellectualism, and stifles conversations about important social issues by dismissing them with a single pejorative label.

II. Concepts and Beliefs

"American Exceptionalism" (Uncritical)
Explanation:
The belief that the United States is uniquely virtuous and superior to other nations, used to justify unilateralism, dismiss international criticism, and avoid critical self-reflection.
Examples:
"America is the greatest country in the world, period!"; "We are exceptional and shouldn't listen to criticism from other nations."
Characteristic Use:
Uncritical "American Exceptionalism" promotes nationalistic arrogance, discourages international cooperation, and hinders self-improvement by fostering complacency.
Risk:
The concept leads to isolationism, hinders diplomatic efforts, and prevents addressing internal problems by fostering a belief in inherent superiority and an unwillingness to learn from others.
"Biblical Literalism" (Selective)
Explanation:
Selectively interpreting the Bible literally to support specific political or social agendas, while ignoring or reinterpreting passages that contradict those agendas, used to justify discriminatory views and policies.
Examples:
"The Bible clearly condemns homosexuality!"; "The Bible supports our political positions." (Ignoring passages about compassion, forgiveness, etc.)
Characteristic Use:
Selective "Biblical literalism" weaponizes religion to justify pre-determined political stances and moral judgments, often ignoring broader theological context and ethical principles.
Risk:
The concept promotes religious dogmatism, intolerance, and the misuse of faith for political purposes, undermining genuine spiritual inquiry and ethical reflection.
"Free Market Fundamentalism" (Unregulated)
Explanation:
The belief that unregulated free markets are the solution to all problems and inherently moral, used to oppose government regulation, social safety nets, and environmental protections, often ignoring market failures and social costs.
Examples:
"The free market will solve everything!"; "Government regulation is always bad for the economy."
Characteristic Use:
Unregulated "free market fundamentalism" prioritizes economic deregulation above all else, often at the expense of social and environmental well-being.
Risk:
The concept leads to increased economic inequality, environmental degradation, and social instability by ignoring the need for regulation and social safety nets to mitigate market failures.
"Trickle-Down Economics" (Disproven)
Explanation:
The discredited theory that tax cuts for the wealthy will "trickle down" to benefit everyone, used to justify policies that exacerbate wealth inequality despite lacking empirical support.
Examples:
"Tax cuts for the rich will create jobs for everyone!"; "Lowering taxes on corporations will boost the economy for all."
Characteristic Use:
"Trickle-down economics" is used to justify policies that primarily benefit the wealthy, despite evidence that they do not effectively benefit the broader population.
Risk:
The concept increases wealth inequality, weakens social mobility, and undermines economic fairness by concentrating wealth at the top without generating widespread prosperity.
"Strong Borders" (Extreme)
Explanation:
Advocating for extreme border security measures, often dehumanizing immigrants and disregarding humanitarian concerns, used to justify harsh immigration policies and promote xenophobia.
Examples:
"We need to seal our borders completely!"; "Strong borders are essential for national security and identity." (Ignoring humanitarian crises and economic benefits of immigration)
Characteristic Use:
Extreme "strong borders" ideology prioritizes exclusion and nationalistic fervor over humanitarianism and nuanced immigration policies.
Risk:
The concept leads to inhumane treatment of immigrants, undermines international law and cooperation, and can damage economic and social well-being by isolating nations and promoting xenophobia.
"Personal Responsibility" (Weaponized)
Explanation:
Overemphasizing individual responsibility to downplay systemic inequalities and social determinants of outcomes, used to justify reduced social safety nets and blame individuals for systemic problems.
Examples:
"People just need to take personal responsibility for their lives!"; "Poverty is a result of individual choices, not systemic issues." (Ignoring factors like lack of opportunity, systemic discrimination)
Characteristic Use:
Weaponized "personal responsibility" deflects attention from systemic issues and blames individuals for problems often rooted in societal structures and inequalities.
Risk:
The concept undermines empathy and social solidarity, justifies inaction on systemic problems, and perpetuates inequality by ignoring structural barriers and social determinants of success.
"Traditional Values" (Vague and Exclusionary)
Explanation:
Appealing to vaguely defined "traditional values" to oppose social change and justify discriminatory views, often selectively invoking historical norms while ignoring others, used to resist progress and maintain social hierarchies.
Examples:
"We need to get back to traditional values!"; "Traditional marriage is the only real marriage." (Ignoring evolving societal norms and diverse family structures)
Characteristic Use:
"Traditional values" rhetoric is used to resist social progress and justify discriminatory views by appealing to a romanticized and selective vision of the past.
Risk:
The concept promotes intolerance, hinders social progress, and often serves to justify discriminatory practices against marginalized groups in the name of an undefined and often exclusionary "tradition."
"Culture War" Mentality (Pervasive)
Explanation:
A pervasive belief that society is engaged in a fundamental conflict between opposing cultural values, used to justify aggressive political tactics, demonize opponents, and resist compromise.
Examples:
"Everything is a battle in the culture war!"; "We must fight the culture war to save our way of life."
Characteristic Use:
Pervasive "culture war" mentality fosters a constant state of conflict and hostility, undermining civil discourse and cooperation across ideological divides.
Risk:
The concept exacerbates social polarization, prevents constructive dialogue and compromise, and can justify anti-democratic actions in the name of winning a perceived existential cultural battle.
"Anti-Globalism" (Extreme)
Explanation:
Extreme rejection of international cooperation and global interconnectedness, often bordering on xenophobia and conspiracy thinking, used to justify isolationist policies and distrust of international institutions.
Examples:
"Globalism is a threat to national sovereignty!"; "International organizations are part of a globalist plot." (Ignoring benefits of trade, diplomacy, etc.)
Characteristic Use:
Extreme "anti-globalism" promotes isolationism, xenophobia, and distrust of international cooperation, often fueled by conspiracy theories.
Risk:
The concept undermines international diplomacy, hinders efforts to address global challenges, and can lead to economic and political isolation, damaging national and global well-being.
Belief in Conspiracy Theories (Widespread)
Explanation:
Widespread acceptance and promotion of conspiracy theories, often without evidence, used to explain complex events, demonize opponents, and undermine trust in established institutions and facts.
Examples:
"The election was stolen!"; "Climate change is a hoax!"; "The Deep State is controlling everything!"
Characteristic Use:
Widespread belief in conspiracy theories undermines rational discourse, critical thinking, and trust in reliable sources of information, creating an alternate reality.
Risk:
The concept erodes trust in institutions, promotes misinformation and disinformation, and can incite violence and social instability by fostering paranoia and rejecting evidence-based reality.
Climate Change Denial/Minimization
Explanation:
Rejecting or minimizing the scientific consensus on climate change, often to protect vested interests or ideological commitments, used to justify inaction on environmental protection and undermine climate policies.
Examples:
"Climate change is a hoax!"; "It's just natural climate variability!"; "Climate action will destroy the economy!" (Ignoring overwhelming scientific evidence)
Characteristic Use:
Climate change denial/minimization prioritizes short-term economic interests or ideological positions over scientific consensus and long-term environmental sustainability.
Risk:
The concept hinders action on climate change, exacerbates environmental crises, and undermines public health and global well-being by rejecting scientific evidence and promoting inaction.
"Election Fraud" Claims (Baseless)
Explanation:
Promoting unsubstantiated claims of widespread election fraud to delegitimize democratic processes and election results, used to undermine faith in democracy and justify authoritarian tendencies.
Examples:
"The election was rigged!"; "There was massive voter fraud!" (Without credible evidence)
Characteristic Use:
Baseless "election fraud" claims are used to undermine democratic legitimacy, sow distrust in elections, and justify efforts to restrict voting rights.
Risk:
The concept erodes faith in democratic institutions, promotes political instability, and can incite political violence by rejecting legitimate election outcomes and undermining the foundations of democracy.
"Deep State" Belief (Paranoid)
Explanation:
A paranoid belief in a hidden, malevolent force within government secretly controlling events, used to justify distrust of government and radical anti-establishment views, often fueled by misinformation and conspiracy theories.
Examples:
"The deep state is out to get us!"; "They are manipulating everything from behind the scenes!" (Without factual basis)
Characteristic Use:
Paranoid "deep state" belief fosters extreme distrust in government and promotes a conspiratorial worldview, undermining rational engagement with political processes.
Risk:
The concept leads to political alienation, undermines civic participation, and can justify violence and extremism by creating a climate of paranoia and distrust of legitimate authority.
"Manosphere" / Anti-Feminist Ideas
Explanation:
Beliefs promoting misogyny, male victimhood, and resentment towards women and feminism, used to justify sexism, online harassment, and opposition to gender equality, often fueled by misinformation and resentment.
Examples:
"Feminism is destroying men!"; "Women are naturally inferior!"; "Men are the real victims of society!" (Based on misogynistic stereotypes and misinformation)
Characteristic Use:
"Manosphere" and anti-feminist ideas promote sexism, misogyny, and hostility towards women, undermining gender equality and fostering harmful gender stereotypes.
Risk:
The concept fuels online harassment, domestic violence, and broader gender inequality, damaging social cohesion and undermining the well-being of both women and men through toxic gender roles and harmful ideologies.

III. Patterns and Tactics

Fear-Mongering
Explanation:
Using exaggerated threats and anxieties to manipulate public opinion and mobilize political support, often relying on misinformation and emotional appeals rather than facts.
Examples:
"Immigrants are invading and will destroy our culture!"; "Socialism will lead to the downfall of America!"
Characteristic Use:
Fear-mongering is used to bypass rational thought and decision-making, promoting emotional reactions and knee-jerk responses to complex issues.
Risk:
The tactic undermines reasoned discourse, promotes social division and anxiety, and can justify discriminatory policies and actions based on unfounded fears.
Demonization of Opponents
Explanation:
Portraying political opponents as evil, un-American, or enemies, used to shut down dialogue, justify aggressive tactics, and dehumanize those with differing viewpoints, undermining civility and compromise.
Examples:
"Democrats are traitors!"; "Liberals hate America!"; "Anyone who disagrees with us is evil."
Characteristic Use:
Demonization of opponents creates an "us vs. them" mentality, making compromise and cooperation impossible and fostering a climate of hostility and intolerance.
Risk:
The tactic erodes civility, undermines democratic processes, and can incite violence and extremism by dehumanizing political opponents and framing disagreements as existential battles.
Whataboutism
Explanation:
Deflecting criticism by pointing to the perceived flaws of opponents or other groups, rather than addressing the original issue, used to avoid accountability and muddy the waters in debates.
Examples:
"Why are you criticizing Trump? What about Obama's scandals?"; "Yes, we did that, but the left does it too!"
Characteristic Use:
Whataboutism is used to deflect criticism and avoid taking responsibility by changing the subject and focusing on perceived hypocrisy or wrongdoing of others.
Risk:
The tactic undermines honest debate, prevents accountability, and obfuscates the original issue by creating a smokescreen of irrelevant counter-accusations.
Straw Man Arguments
Explanation:
Misrepresenting an opponent's argument to make it easier to attack, used to discredit opposing viewpoints through distortion and exaggeration, rather than engaging with their actual claims.
Examples:
"Democrats want to ban all guns!"; "Liberals want to destroy the nuclear family!" (Misrepresenting nuanced positions)
Characteristic Use:
Straw man arguments are used to simplify and distort opposing viewpoints, making them appear extreme or ridiculous and easier to dismiss without genuine engagement.
Risk:
The tactic undermines honest debate, prevents understanding of opposing viewpoints, and promotes misinformation and polarization by relying on misrepresentation rather than reasoned argument.
Dog Whistling
Explanation:
Using coded language that has a specific meaning for a targeted subgroup while appearing innocuous to the general public, used to appeal to prejudiced groups without openly expressing discriminatory views.
Examples:
"States' rights" (historically used to defend segregation); "Law and order" (often used to target minority communities); "Globalist" (often with anti-Semitic undertones).
Characteristic Use:
Dog whistling allows for the dissemination of prejudiced views and appeals to discriminatory sentiments while maintaining plausible deniability and avoiding direct condemnation.
Risk:
The tactic normalizes prejudice, allows for the spread of hateful ideologies, and undermines honest and transparent political discourse by relying on coded language and hidden meanings.
False Equivalence
Explanation:
Presenting two unequal or unrelated things as equally valid or comparable, used to downplay wrongdoing or create a false sense of balance where none exists, often to minimize own failings or exaggerate opponent's.
Examples:
"Both sides are equally corrupt!"; "Violence on the left and right is the same!"; "Both parties are equally extreme!" (Ignoring vast differences in scale or nature)
Characteristic Use:
False equivalence is used to obfuscate differences, downplay wrongdoing, and create a misleading sense of balance or moral equivalency where it is not warranted.
Risk:
The tactic undermines critical thinking, distorts reality, and prevents accountability by blurring lines between right and wrong and creating a false sense of moral relativism.
Manufacturing Consent
Explanation:
Systematically shaping public opinion through media manipulation and propaganda, used to create artificial support for specific policies or agendas, often through repetition, framing, and selective information.
Examples:
Fox News consistently promoting a specific political narrative; Right-wing media outlets amplifying misinformation about elections.
Characteristic Use:
Manufacturing consent is used to manipulate public opinion and create a false sense of popular support for specific agendas, often through coordinated media campaigns and propaganda.
Risk:
The tactic undermines informed democratic participation, erodes trust in media, and can lead to public support for harmful policies based on manipulated perceptions rather than genuine understanding.
Propaganda (Overt and Covert)
Explanation:
Spreading biased or misleading information to promote a specific political cause or viewpoint, can be overt (obvious and direct) or covert (subtle and manipulative), used to influence beliefs and behaviors.
Examples:
Overt propaganda: State-controlled media directly promoting government narratives; Covert propaganda: Social media campaigns spreading disinformation disguised as organic content.
Characteristic Use:
Propaganda is used to manipulate public opinion, control narratives, and create support for specific ideologies or political actions, often through emotional appeals and misinformation.
Risk:
The tactic undermines informed decision-making, erodes trust in information sources, and can lead to public acceptance of harmful ideologies and policies based on manipulated perceptions rather than facts.
Misinformation/Disinformation (Deliberate)
Explanation:
Spreading false or misleading information, can be unintentional (misinformation) or deliberate (disinformation), used to confuse, deceive, and manipulate public opinion for political or ideological gain.
Examples:
Spreading false claims about election fraud; Sharing fabricated stories about political opponents; Deliberately distorting scientific data to fit a political agenda.
Characteristic Use:
Misinformation and disinformation are used to undermine trust in facts and evidence, create confusion, and manipulate public opinion by flooding the information space with false or misleading content.
Risk:
The tactics erode trust in credible sources of information, undermine informed decision-making, and can incite violence and social instability by promoting false narratives and conspiracy theories.
"Culture Wars" as a Tactic
Explanation:
Intentionally creating and escalating social and cultural conflicts for political gain, used to mobilize base, distract from other issues, and polarize the electorate, often through wedge issues and moral panics.
Examples:
Focusing on "bathroom bills" or "critical race theory" debates to rally conservative voters; Exaggerating threats to "traditional values" to create moral panics.
Characteristic Use:
"Culture wars" are strategically employed as a political tactic to divide the electorate, mobilize specific voting blocs, and distract from other policy issues through manufactured social conflicts.
Risk:
The tactic exacerbates social divisions, undermines social cohesion, and prevents addressing real societal problems by focusing on manufactured conflicts and culture clashes rather than substantive policy debates.
"Us vs. Them" Framing (Pervasive)
Explanation:
Consistently framing issues as a battle between "us" (good, patriotic) and "them" (bad, un-American), used to create in-group loyalty, demonize opponents, and shut down nuanced thinking or compromise.
Examples:
"Real Americans vs. the liberal elite"; "Patriots vs. traitors"; "Conservatives vs. socialists."
Characteristic Use:
Pervasive "us vs. them" framing simplifies complex issues into simplistic binaries, fosters tribalism and hostility, and prevents constructive dialogue or compromise across ideological lines.
Risk:
The tactic undermines social cohesion, exacerbates political polarization, and can incite violence and extremism by dehumanizing out-groups and framing political disagreements as existential conflicts.
Appeals to Tradition (Uncritical)
Explanation:
Justifying policies or views simply because they are "traditional" or "how things have always been done," used to resist progress, defend outdated practices, and ignore the potential for improvement or changing societal needs.
Examples:
"Marriage should only be between a man and a woman because that's tradition!"; "We should keep doing things this way because that's how it's always been done." (Ignoring societal evolution and changing values)
Characteristic Use:
Uncritical appeals to tradition are used to resist social change and defend the status quo, often ignoring the historical context and potential harms associated with outdated practices.
Risk:
The tactic hinders social progress, justifies inequality and discrimination, and prevents adaptation to changing circumstances by romanticizing the past and rejecting the need for evolution and reform.
Religious Dogmatism (Political Weapon)
Explanation:
Using religious beliefs as unquestionable justification for political positions, used to shut down debate, claim moral superiority, and mobilize religious voters through fear and moralistic appeals, undermining secular governance.
Examples:
"My religion says this is wrong, so it must be illegal!"; "God is on our side in this political battle!" (Ignoring diverse religious views and secular ethics)
Characteristic Use:
Religious dogmatism weaponizes faith for political purposes, framing political agendas as divinely ordained and opponents as morally deficient or against God's will.
Risk:
The tactic undermines secular governance, promotes religious intolerance and discrimination, and hinders reasoned debate by claiming divine authority for political viewpoints.
Conspiracy Theories as Political Tool
Explanation:
Promoting and spreading conspiracy theories for political purposes, used to undermine trust in institutions, demonize opponents, and mobilize base through fear and paranoia, regardless of factual basis.
Examples:
QAnon and other political conspiracy movements; Spreading false claims about election rigging or "deep state" plots.
Characteristic Use:
Conspiracy theories are weaponized to create political chaos, undermine trust in established systems, and mobilize extremist groups through fear and misinformation.
Risk:
The tactic erodes trust in institutions, promotes social division and instability, and can incite violence and extremism by fostering paranoia and rejecting evidence-based reality in favor of baseless narratives.
Attacks on Experts/Intellectuals
Explanation:
Discrediting experts, scientists, academics, and intellectuals who disagree with political positions, used to undermine evidence-based reasoning, promote anti-intellectualism, and elevate gut feelings or ideological dogma over expertise.
Examples:
"Scientists are just part of the liberal elite!"; "Academics are out of touch with the real world!"; "Experts are always wrong!" (Dismissing scientific consensus, academic research)
Characteristic Use:
Attacks on experts are used to undermine trust in credible sources of information and elevate ideological dogma or gut feelings over evidence and reasoned analysis.
Risk:
The tactic promotes anti-intellectualism, hinders informed decision-making, and undermines public trust in expertise and evidence-based knowledge, leading to flawed policies and a rejection of reason.
Cult of Personality (Leader-Worship)
Explanation:
Promoting unquestioning loyalty and devotion to a political leader, often bordering on authoritarianism, used to consolidate power, suppress dissent, and create a movement centered around an individual rather than principles or policies.
Examples:
Trump rallies and unwavering support regardless of actions or statements; Deification of political figures and unquestioning obedience to their pronouncements.
Characteristic Use:
Cult of personality tactics are used to create unquestioning loyalty to a leader, suppressing dissent and undermining democratic accountability by prioritizing personal devotion over critical thinking.
Risk:
The tactic undermines democratic norms and institutions, can lead to authoritarianism, and discourages critical thinking and independent judgment, fostering blind obedience and suppressing dissent.
Echo Chamber Creation/Maintenance (Right-Wing Media)
Explanation:
Creating and maintaining media ecosystems where only certain viewpoints are amplified and dissenting opinions are excluded or demonized, used to reinforce ideological conformity and insulate adherents from critical perspectives.
Examples:
Fox News, Breitbart, and other right-wing media outlets creating a self-contained information bubble; Social media algorithms reinforcing echo chambers by showing users only content they agree with.
Characteristic Use:
Right-wing media echo chambers are used to reinforce ideological conformity, limit exposure to diverse viewpoints, and create a distorted perception of reality within a closed information ecosystem.
Risk:
The tactic undermines critical thinking, promotes groupthink, and exacerbates political polarization by creating information silos where dissenting viewpoints are silenced and biased narratives are reinforced without challenge.
Thought-Terminating Cliches (Right-Wing)
Explanation:
Using simplistic slogans and catchphrases to shut down critical thinking and debate, used to oversimplify complex issues, promote emotional responses over reasoned analysis, and reinforce ideological dogma.
Examples:
"Make America Great Again!"; "Build the Wall!"; "Lock Her Up!"; "Socialism bad, Freedom good!"; "Don't tread on me!"
Characteristic Use:
Right-wing thought-terminating cliches are used to simplify complex issues, evoke emotional responses, and shut down nuanced debate by relying on simplistic slogans and emotionally charged catchphrases.
Risk:
The tactic undermines critical thinking, hinders nuanced understanding, and promotes simplistic and emotionally driven responses to complex social and political issues, preventing reasoned discourse and effective problem-solving.
Loaded Language/Buzzwords (Right-Wing)
Explanation:
